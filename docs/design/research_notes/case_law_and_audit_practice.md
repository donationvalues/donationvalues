# Case law and IRS audit practice on valuing donated used clothing and household goods

Research snapshot dated 2026-11-02. Scope: US federal; individual donors of tangible personal property (clothing, household goods, furniture, books, toys) to thrift-type charities. Primary-source status is flagged per finding: most Tax Court memorandum/summary opinions could not be fetched from ustaxcourt.gov (DAWSON) or the usual mirrors (casetext gone; CourtListener API 401; GiftLaw Pro and Tax Notes 403), so many case facts come from practitioner summaries and the IRS Chief Counsel case list. Where I could read opinion text directly (Besaw 2025, Stamoulis 2007, Haag 2016 via quoted excerpts, Copeland (Or. Tax Ct.) 2025) I say so.

Cross-cutting conclusion for DonationValues.org design: no federal court has rejected a per-item value *because it came from a Goodwill/Salvation Army/ItsDeductible-style guide*; every clothing/household case in the record was lost on substantiation (no contemporaneous written acknowledgment describing the items, no itemized list, no cost basis/acquisition data for the >$500 tier, no qualified appraisal once "similar items" aggregate above $5,000) or on an obviously wrong market (original retail price, replacement cost new). The IRS's own Form 8283 instructions list "Thrift shop value" as an acceptable entry for the method used to determine FMV for clothing and household items, and Pub 561/526 define the relevant market as what buyers actually pay in consignment or thrift shops.

---

## Key Question 1: Key cases — citations, facts, holdings, reasoning

### Takeaway
Every reported federal case involving bulk donations of used clothing/household goods to Goodwill, Salvation Army, AMVETS, Purple Heart, Housing Works, church flea markets, etc. was lost (or reduced to the IRS-allowed amount) on substantiation grounds; the only case in which a court independently set a value for used clothing (Stamoulis, 2007 summary opinion) benchmarked against "the average for similarly situated taxpayers" rather than any guide, and expressly said resale prices of the donated items "would certainly be relevant and persuasive evidence."

### Cited Findings

**Kunkel v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2015-71 (Judge Lauber), tax year 2011**
- Facts: married couple claimed $37,315 noncash deduction. $13,115 of items allegedly given to Upper Dublin Lutheran Church's 2011 flea market: books ($8,000), household items ($1,303), clothing ($1,000), toys ($822), telescopes ($800), jewelry ($780), household furniture ($410); no receipt or acknowledgment from the church. $24,200 to Goodwill, Purple Heart and Vietnam Veterans of America: clothing ($20,920), household furniture ($2,680), household items ($350), toys ($250); taxpayers could not say which items went to which charity. Goodwill drop-offs were left at unattended warehouse hours in batches they believed were under $250 to avoid needing receipts. Purple Heart / Vietnam Veterans pickups were documented only by undated doorknob hangers reading "Thank you for your contribution." The only itemized list was a spreadsheet created during the IRS audit (e.g., "67 blouses," "100 baby outfits") with "estimated amounts" as FMV; the taxpayers testified they had kept index cards but never produced them. — [Church Law & Tax summary](https://www.churchlawandtax.com/legal-developments/married-couples-37315-charitable-contribution-deduction-disallowed/)
- IRS Chief Counsel's own case digest: "TPs claimed noncash contributions in TY 2011 in amount of $37,000. Except for a spreadsheet they created during the audit and undated doorknob hangers left by Purple Heart and Vietnam Veterans (saying thank you for your contribution) TPs had no substantiation or receipts or CWAs for the noncash contributions. Holding: no deduction, because no CWA, receipts not adequate, did not present credible evidence that the allegedly donated household items were in good used condition or better in accordance with section 170(f)(16)(C)." — [IRS CCA 202020002, "Select Charitable Contribution Cases 2012 Forward," item 54](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-wd/202020002.pdf)
- Court quoted: "The court has no doubt that the taxpayers did donate some property to charitable organizations during 2011" but "Because the taxpayers did not satisfy these requirements, we are unable to allow a deduction for their claimed noncash gifts." Failure of the $250 CWA tier "was fatal to their claim." For the >$500 tier the taxpayers kept no written records of acquisition dates, manner of acquisition, cost basis, FMV, or valuation method. — [Church Law & Tax](https://www.churchlawandtax.com/legal-developments/married-couples-37315-charitable-contribution-deduction-disallowed/)
- Secondary summaries report the court applied the similar-items aggregation rule to the $500 and $5,000 thresholds and sustained a 20% accuracy penalty because the taxpayers kept no records (no reasonable-cause defense). Deficiency reported as $12,388 (one source) or $12,338 (another); penalty $2,468. — [search summary of GiftLaw/Dykema/CALT coverage](https://legacy.jwu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=376); [Dykema](https://www.dykema.com/news-insights/united-states-tax-court-ruling-highlights-importance-of-record-keeping-for-charitable-contributions.html)
- A practitioner summary (Patch.com) says the court aggregated the Goodwill-type donations above the $5,000 threshold and "placed the burden on the taxpayer to obtain documentation for items left at unattended drop sites." — [Patch](https://patch.com/south-carolina/mountpleasant-sc/irs-charitable-deduction-audit)
- Iowa State CALT's annotation (snippet only; page fetch failed on a certificate error) says Judge Lauber, citing *Gaerttner v. Commissioner*, T.C. Memo. 2012-43, listed the minimum records the Kunkels should have had before filing, including the date and manner of acquisition. — [CALT](https://www.calt.iastate.edu/annotation/gift-giver-gets-no-deduction-and-nice-penalty-return)

**Ohde v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2017-137 (Judge Lauber), Docket 11688-15, filed July 10, 2017, tax year 2011**
- IRS determined a deficiency of $32,964 and a §6662(a) penalty of $6,593; issue was whether petitioners could deduct $145,250 rather than the $250 the IRS allowed. Property was donated to Goodwill Industries in Frederick, Maryland. — [Leagle (opening of opinion only)](https://www.leagle.com/decision/intco20170710f55)
- Facts per CPA Practice Advisor: Shepherdstown, WV couple claimed more than 20,000 items over "dozens of trips." About half the claimed value was clothing and accessories; furniture and 3,153 books made up much of the rest. Goodwill issued a one-page printed receipt per delivery listing only broad categories (clothing, shoes, media, furniture, household items) with no item descriptions, counts or condition. The main evidence was a spreadsheet generated by TurboTax's ItsDeductible program listing item types, counts, and a "quality" rating — every item rated "high" — with no cost basis; it was not prepared at the time of the gifts. After trial they submitted a second spreadsheet assigning dollar values, with no evidence of how the values were determined. The court did not find the taxpayers credible, limited the deduction to $250, and imposed the accuracy penalty. — [CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2017/07/25/tax-court-denies-deduction-for-donation-of-20000-household-items/26389/)
- Reported as Mark Robert Ohde and Rose M. Ohde v. Commissioner, 114 T.C.M. 41; the couple owed "almost $40,000 in deficiencies and related penalties." — [CPA Practice Advisor (July 27, 2017)](https://cpapracticeadvisor.com/2017/07/27/irs-audit-taxpayers-claim-145000-income-tax-deduction-for-donations-to-goodwill/26393/)
- The Tax Adviser (2019) summarizes: deduction of $145,250 for over 20,000 Goodwill items disallowed "for inadequate receipts, no credible valuation evidence, and missing appraisals and donee/appraiser signatures" on the Forms 8283 for groups over $5,000. — [The Tax Adviser, Mar. 2019](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2019/mar/potential-pitfalls-charitable-contribution-substantiation-reporting/)

**Grainger v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2018-117, tax year 2012**
- Facts: retired taxpayer bought heavily marked-down, mostly out-of-season Talbots clothing and gave it to Goodwill, claiming deductions at the original price tags: $18,288 (2010), $32,672 (2011), $34,401 (2012). For 2012 she paid $6,047 out of pocket ($2,520 cash + $3,527 Talbots loyalty points). She attached six Forms 8283 listing dresses, jackets etc., various Goodwill centers as donees, and "FMV" as the valuation method; no Goodwill official signed them. IRS allowed $2,520; Appeals raised it to $6,117 (adding loyalty points to basis). — [The Tax Adviser, Oct. 2018](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2018/oct/inflated-charitable-contribution-deduction-denied.html)
- Valuation holding: the court rejected the view that FMV equals the original price tag under the willing-buyer/willing-seller test; "no rational buyer having knowledge of the relevant facts would have paid for these items a price higher than the price Talbots was then charging" Grainger. — [GiftLaw Pro summary](https://plannedgiving.mssu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=460); [Lerch Early](https://www.lerchearly.com/news/donation-of-discounted-goods-cannot-get-list-price-deduction-grainger-tcm-2018-117/)
- Substantiation holdings: all donated items were clothing, so they were "similar items of property" under Reg. 1.170A-13(c)(7)(iii) and aggregated; the combined claim exceeded $5,000, triggering the qualified-appraisal and appraisal-summary requirements, and she had no appraisal. Forms 8283 were invalid because Goodwill did not execute them. Goodwill's receipts were not valid CWAs under §170(f)(8) because they noted clothing donations but did not identify specific items or the number donated per visit. Her self-prepared spreadsheet got no evidentiary weight. Footnote: even with a higher valuation, §170(e)(1)(A) would reduce the deduction to cost basis because the items were donated soon after purchase (short-term gain property). Court said she had fallen "far short of substantiating noncash charitable contributions in excess of the amount respondent has allowed." — [The Tax Adviser, Oct. 2018](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2018/oct/inflated-charitable-contribution-deduction-denied.html)
- The court characterized her arrangement as a "personal tax shelter" (per secondary summaries). — [GiftLaw Pro snippet](https://plannedgiving.mssu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=460)

**Smith (Thad DeShawn) v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2014-203 (Oct. 2, 2014)**
- Claimed deduction for gifts of furniture and clothing to AMVETS valued at $27,767; taxpayer used a Salvation Army website valuation guide to set values; no qualified appraisal; court treated the deduction as unsubstantiated "and therefore valued at zero"; §6662(a) penalty sustained. The AMVETS receipts were signed before the property was allegedly donated, so the court questioned whether they were an "acknowledgment" at all. Court suggested all furniture and clothing items would be aggregated for the $5,000 threshold. — [search summary of GiftLaw Pro ID=363 and Keiter CPA](https://plannedgiving.mssu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=363); [Keiter CPA](https://keitercpa.com/blog/case-importance-substantiation-claiming-charitable-deductions/)
- IRS Chief Counsel digest: "Deduction in excess of $500 claimed for contributions of clothing or household items but TP presented no evidence that they were in 'good used condition or better' and did not furnish a qualified appraisal with his return. Petitioner claimed deduction in excess of $5000 without obtaining a qualified appraisal or attaching a fully completed appraisal summary. Acknowledgment did not constitute CWA." — [IRS CCA 202020002, item 49](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-wd/202020002.pdf)
- Note: this is the only case found in which a court addressed values drawn from a Salvation Army guide; the guide values were never reached because the appraisal/CWA failures were dispositive.

**Bass (Duncan) v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2023-41 (Judge Ashford), Docket 833-20, filed Mar. 27, 2023, tax year 2017**
- Facts: North Carolina taxpayer running landscaping, janitorial and used-clothing operations donated men's, women's and children's clothing plus some nonclothing items to Goodwill and the Salvation Army in 173 separate trips (one source says 172), often several per day, keeping each receipt under $250 in the belief this avoided appraisals. He acquired the items free from residential clients. A charity worker gave him a receipt for each trip; he filled in the items and FMVs himself. Goodwill receipts totaled $18,837 (incl. $13,852 clothing and household items); Salvation Army receipts $11,779 (incl. $11,594 clothing). Two Forms 8283 attached; no appraisal. — [CPA Practice Advisor, May 2023](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2023/05/16/sizing-up-a-charitable-deduction-for-clothing-when-is-an-appraisal-required/79750/); [GiftLaw Pro ID=583 (snippet)](https://plannedgiving.mssu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=583)
- Holding: he argued no single item exceeded $5,000; the court held similar items must be aggregated whether donated to one charity or several, so clothing over $5,000 in the aggregate required a qualified appraisal. The charity receipts did not qualify as a "qualified appraisal or statement of value"; each receipt left the valuation section blank, leaving valuation to the taxpayer. Entire property deduction denied; the Tax Court "sustained the majority of an IRS deficiency and penalty." — [J.K. Lasser](https://www.jklasser.com/news/when-an-appraisal-for-donations-of-used-clothing-is-required/); [CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2023/05/16/sizing-up-a-charitable-deduction-for-clothing-when-is-an-appraisal-required/79750/); [Bradford Tax Institute teaser](https://blog.bradfordtaxinstitute.com/why-did-duncan-bass-make-172-trips-to-goodwill-and-the-salvation-army)
- Commentator's practice note: "the IRS doesn't specify amounts so you can rely on guidelines provided by organizations like Goodwill and the Salvation Army." — [CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2023/05/16/sizing-up-a-charitable-deduction-for-clothing-when-is-an-appraisal-required/79750/)

**Besaw (John Henry) v. Commissioner, T.C. Summary Opinion 2025-7, Docket 19222-22S, filed July 21, 2025 (Special Trial Judge Leyden)** — read directly from the opinion PDF
- Facts: 2019 joint return claimed $6,760 noncash contributions; Form 8283 with continuation sheets identified donees with addresses and short item descriptions but "did not include dates of the donations or the values of those donated items." During exam he submitted receipts "dated and signed by employees of the donee organizations" but "The sections on these receipts to identify the goods donated and their values were blank," plus a noncontemporaneous document titled "2019 Reconstructed from Form 8283 and Continuation Sheet" listing donees, dates, descriptions and "cost/current value," prepared after filing and sent to the IRS in 2022. — [Opinion PDF via Bradford Tax Institute](https://bradfordtaxinstitute.com/Endnotes/TC_Summ_Op_2025-7.pdf)
- The IRS's notice of deficiency explanation (useful as a statement of what examiners require): "To be allowed a deduction for property as a contribution, you must show (a) the name and address of the qualifying organization(s), (b) provide a list of what was donated, and (c) show the fair market value of each item on the date of contribution. Since you have not met these requirements, we have adjusted the amount as shown." Deficiency $2,626; §6662(a) penalty $525 (penalty later conceded by IRS). — [Opinion PDF](https://bradfordtaxinstitute.com/Endnotes/TC_Summ_Op_2025-7.pdf)
- Legal standard restated: "Noncash contributions must be evidenced by a receipt from the donee organization. See Treas. Reg. § 1.170A-13(b)(1). The receipt must show (1) the name of the donee organization; (2) the date and location of the contribution; and (3) the property description in detail reasonably sufficient under the circumstances." The reg "further explains that '[a]lthough the fair market value of the property is one of the circumstances to be taken into account in determining the amount of detail to be included on the receipt, such value need not be stated on the receipt.'" For >$500: "the taxpayer must also maintain written records establishing (1) the item's manner of acquisition as well as either the item's approximate date of acquisition ... and (2) the cost or other basis." — [Opinion PDF](https://bradfordtaxinstitute.com/Endnotes/TC_Summ_Op_2025-7.pdf)
- Holding: "Although the Court believes petitioner donated items to charitable organizations in 2019, the record before the Court shows that petitioner did not satisfy the substantiation requirements ... Regardless of whether petitioner met the requirements for deducting charitable contributions of $250 or more, or for deducting charitable contributions of property valued in excess of $500, none of the receipts he provided from the charitable organizations for his donations included any descriptions of the donated items; therefore, petitioner is not entitled to deduct noncash charitable contributions for tax year 2019." — [Opinion PDF](https://bradfordtaxinstitute.com/Endnotes/TC_Summ_Op_2025-7.pdf); commentary at [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/08/taxpayer-loses-charitable-deduction-due-to-lack-of-documentation)

**Gibson (Luke and Breeana) v. Commissioner, T.C. Summary Opinion 2026-1 (Jan. 6, 2026), tax year 2019**
- Over 4,000 items of "high end cycling EQ apparel" (said to be a gift from a family member) donated to Hiway 80 Rescue Mission Ministry; Schedule A charitable deduction $194,273, of which $188,563 was the apparel; Form 8283 listed donor's cost $251,417, FMV $188,563, and "Appraisal" as the method, but no appraisal was attached. An appraisal by Eugene H. Ruelle dated March 26, 2019 and a same-date donee receipt were submitted with an amended return during exam; IRS did not accept it. Court: CWA requirement satisfied by the donee receipt, but the >$500 tier failed (vague testimony on acquisition; "the law requires detailed substantiation of the basis of the property") and the >$5,000 tier failed ("the record does not confirm that Mr. Ruelle was a qualified appraiser"). Full $188,563 disallowed. — [Current Federal Tax Developments](https://www.currentfederaltaxdevelopments.com/blog/2026/1/6/strict-adherence-to-substantiation-requirements-an-analysis-of-gibson-v-commissioner)
- Gibson gave "confusing testimony suggesting he thought the deductible amount was the fair market value discounted by 25-50%," which the court found unsupported. — [search summary of CPA Practice Advisor / NC Bar Blog coverage](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2026/02/13/tax-court-slams-brakes-on-charitable-deduction/178117/)

**Haag v. Commissioner, T.C. Summary Opinion 2016-29**
- Ms. Haag moved out in early 2012 and gave household items to the Salvation Army and a veterans charity; Form 8283 used "Comparative sales" as the method; claimed clothing $2,082 and furniture/kitchenware $1,087 to Salvation Army plus items to the veterans charity. IRS allowed $250; court sustained: "we sustain respondent's determination limiting petitioners' deduction for noncash charitable contributions to $250." Opinion restates the tiers: receipt must give "a description of the property in detail reasonably sufficient under the circumstances"; over $500 "the taxpayer normally must also maintain written records showing the manner in which the item was acquired." — [Current Federal Tax Developments (quoting opinion)](https://www.currentfederaltaxdevelopments.com/blog/2016/6/22/taxpayers-lose-virtually-all-charitable-contributions-for-failure-to-meet-strict-substantiation-rules-in-the-law-and-regulations)

**Payne v. Commissioner, T.C. Summ. Op. 2016-30**
- Deductions for clothing, furniture and household goods denied beyond the $1,500 per year the IRS allowed; the court "stressed that gifts of clothing, furniture and other household items should be documented by listing a specific number of items." — [GiftLaw Pro ID=416 (snippet via search)](https://plannedgiving.mssu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=416)

**Gaines v. Commissioner, T.C. Summ. Op. 2017-15 (Mar. 16, 2017)**
- $18,000 deduction claimed for clothing reported as given to "Goodwill" but actually given to a church the taxpayer was not sure still existed; no receipt, "no details as to the specific items gifted, no written substantiation and no method for determining the value." No deduction allowed. — [GiftLaw Pro ID=435 (snippet via search)](https://plannedgiving.mssu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=435)

**Roberts (Edmund Douglas) v. Commissioner, T.C. Summ. Op. 2010-76 (June 17, 2010), tax year 2005**
- $28,655 noncash for more than 450 items (mostly used clothing; also towels, bedsheets, books, costume jewelry, toys, glass lamps) on a self-prepared substitute Form 8283 with self-assigned values; five Goodwill receipts, only one signed, with descriptions such as "4 bags of clothes." No appraisal summary; receipts did not reconcile with the Form 8283; disallowed for failure to establish entitlement "by proper and adequate substantiation." Reader comments on the post noted the claim averaged about $64 per item. — [TaxProf Blog](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/?p=7997)

**Stamoulis v. Commissioner, T.C. Summary Opinion (March 2007), tax year 2002** — read from the opinion text (onecle mirror)
- Goldman Sachs investment banker (2002 AGI $114,819) claimed $55,764 charitable deductions: $5,917 cash, $48,954 property, $893 carryover; most property went to Housing Works, "a 'high-end' thrift store in New York City"; she estimated she spent $53,916 on clothing and $9,253 on shoes that year and "wears the items once or twice, and then donates them to an upscale thrift shop." Forms 8283 listed the valuation method as "actual value" or "straight line depreciation." Housing Works' Donation Inventory List is completed by the donor and "Housing Works does not verify the accuracy." IRS initially disallowed everything, then conceded $4,652. — [onecle p.4](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2007/stamoulis.sum.wpd04.html); [p.5](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2007/stamoulis.sum.wpd05.html); [p.6](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2007/stamoulis.sum.wpd06.html)
- Valuation reasoning: the inventory lists "present a fairly accurate description of the items donated"; she estimated FMVs herself, checked by Internet research done for trial; "The actual costs of those items are not taken into account in petitioner's estimates"; she did not provide Housing Works' resale prices or prices of similar items, which "would certainly be relevant and persuasive evidence" of FMV; the court had "severe reservations regarding the fair market values that petitioner assigned." Footnote 14: she first set each item's FMV then assumed original cost was at least twice that, so the Form 8283 costs "are themselves not actual costs, but only estimates." Valuation is "an approximation" and "an inexact science"; used clothing "will be worth far less" than original purchase price immediately after purchase; "We cannot accept petitioner's fair market value estimates of the property." — [onecle p.10](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2007/stamoulis.sum.wpd10.html); [p.11](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2007/stamoulis.sum.wpd11.html)
- Result: the court allowed property contributions of $8,949.15 by "measuring petitioner's claimed deduction against the average for similarly situated taxpayers" (total 2002 deduction $10,002 incl. $1,053 cash); no accuracy penalty because the "optimistic" valuation was not negligence. — [onecle p.12](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2007/stamoulis.sum.wpd12.html); [Accounting Today](https://www.accountingtoday.com/news/tax-court-no-dice-on-49k-clothing-deduction)
- Press coverage: her lawyer said "I don't think the court was very familiar with couture clothing"; a consignment-shop owner quoted in coverage said items typically resell for about 20 percent of the original price. — [Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/thrift-shop-donor-told-to-slash-deduction-claim/); [Accounting Today](https://www.accountingtoday.com/news/tax-court-no-dice-on-49k-clothing-deduction)

**Barnes v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2016-79 (Apr. 27, 2016)**
- Items at issue included designer clothing along with furniture, bedding, kitchenware and other household goods; only fragments of the opinion were retrievable, so the valuation holding is unconfirmed. — [Syfert case page (partial text)](https://syfert.com/caselaw/case.php?id=4563053)

**Copeland v. Department of Revenue, TC-MD 240623R (Oregon Tax Court, Magistrate Division, Sept. 15, 2025)** — state case applying the federal FMV definition; read from vLex text
- Claimed $14,100 noncash (Goodwill and Brightside Animal Center) for clothing, lamps, coffee table, ceiling fans, TV, blender, toaster, vacuum, microwave, books, Tempur-Pedic mattress set, Samsung washer, furniture, toys, prom clothing etc.; spreadsheet used "REPLACEMENT COST NEW" for 34 items and "REPLACEMENT COST USED" for 3; relied on original Amazon prices and current Facebook Marketplace listings. Court: her valuations "do not establish FMV"; replacement cost counts as evidence "only" if it matches "what a willing and knowledgeable buyer would actually pay in the marketplace"; "[c]ourts have consistently recognized that, in general, comparable sales constitute the best evidence of market value"; "replacement cost new is not a reliable measure of FMV" (citing Gordin v. Dept. of Rev.); "The original prices paid to Amazon do not establish current FMV" given age and condition; Facebook comparables rejected because she did not show how they corresponded to her items. The court then turned to §170(f)(11)(F) similar-items aggregation (text truncated). — [vLex](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/copeland-v-dep-t-1091465844); [Bloomberg Tax headline](https://news.bloombergtax.com/daily-tax-report/oregon-taxpayer-denied-14-000-deduction-for-charity-donations)

**Aimiuwu v. Commissioner, No. 5576-24S (small tax case, 2026)** — not a clothing case; $49,986 "Donation" with no receipt or §170(c) donee proof, denied. Included only because it surfaced in 2026 searches. — [Taxcoda](https://taxcoda.com/p/court-denies-couples-charitable-deduction)

### Inferences
- Across roughly fifteen years of decisions (Roberts 2010 through Gibson 2026) the Tax Court has never had to decide whether a *reasonable thrift-value* for a well-documented item was right or wrong, because no taxpayer reached that question with intact substantiation. A dataset that produces defensible numbers is necessary but not sufficient; the product must force the substantiation chain (itemized contemporaneous list, per-visit receipt tied to the list, condition, acquisition info, basis, and an appraisal gate at $5,000 per similar-item category).
- Judges repeatedly say they believe the donations happened (Kunkel, Besaw, Gibson, Smith) and still disallow 100%; the all-or-nothing outcome is driven by §170(f)(8), (f)(11) and (f)(16), not by valuation disputes.
- "Every item rated high quality" (Ohde) and uniformly "good used condition" (Bass) with no cost basis reads as a credibility problem to the court; a guide that forces condition heterogeneity and captures basis/acquisition would look more credible.

## Key Question 2: How courts have treated ItsDeductible, Salvation Army/Goodwill guides, eBay/online values; any guide-based value accepted; any court substitution of "thrift shop value" or a fraction of claimed value

### Takeaway
No federal opinion located accepts or rejects a guide value on its merits: ItsDeductible appeared in Ohde and a Salvation Army guide in Smith, but both cases were decided on missing CWAs/appraisals and lack of basis records, not on the guide numbers; the one court that substituted its own number (Stamoulis) used "the average for similarly situated taxpayers" and said actual resale prices would have been the persuasive evidence, while an Oregon court (Copeland) rejected Amazon-new and Facebook Marketplace comparables that were not matched to the items.

### Cited Findings
- Ohde: the taxpayers' "main evidence was a spreadsheet generated by TurboTax's 'ItsDeductible' program" listing item types, counts and a quality rating ("every item rated 'high'"), no cost basis, not prepared at the time of the gifts and not corroborated; the court did not find the taxpayers credible and cut the deduction to $250. The article does not report the court evaluating ItsDeductible's per-item numbers. — [CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2017/07/25/tax-court-denies-deduction-for-donation-of-20000-household-items/26389/)
- Smith (T.C. Memo. 2014-203): taxpayer "had used a Salvation Army website guide to determine the value" of $27,767 of furniture and clothing; "with no appraisals the deduction was not substantiated and therefore valued at zero." — [search summary of GiftLaw Pro ID=363](https://plannedgiving.mssu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=363)
- Stamoulis: court said Housing Works' resale prices or prices for similar items "would certainly be relevant and persuasive evidence" of FMV; it rejected the donor's own estimates and allowed $8,949.15 by "measuring petitioner's claimed deduction against the average for similarly situated taxpayers." — [onecle p.10](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2007/stamoulis.sum.wpd10.html); [p.12](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2007/stamoulis.sum.wpd12.html)
- Grainger: original retail price tags rejected as FMV; deduction limited to what she actually paid (IRS-allowed $6,117), with the court noting any higher value would anyway be cut to basis under §170(e)(1)(A). — [The Tax Adviser](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2018/oct/inflated-charitable-contribution-deduction-denied.html)
- Copeland (Or. Tax Ct. 2025): "replacement cost new is not a reliable measure of FMV"; Amazon original prices "do not establish current FMV"; Facebook Marketplace listings rejected because the taxpayer "did not show how they corresponded to her items, and several were for items not in her notebook." — [vLex](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/copeland-v-dep-t-1091465844)
- Gibson (2026): taxpayer's belief that the deduction was FMV "discounted by 25-50%" found to have no support. — [search summary of CPA Practice Advisor coverage](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2026/02/13/tax-court-slams-brakes-on-charitable-deduction/178117/)
- IRS allowed amounts in these cases function as de facto "thrift value" floors: $250 (Ohde, Haag), $1,500/yr (Payne), $6,117 = cost (Grainger), $4,652 conceded then $8,949 (Stamoulis). — [CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2017/07/25/tax-court-denies-deduction-for-donation-of-20000-household-items/26389/); [Current Federal Tax Developments](https://www.currentfederaltaxdevelopments.com/blog/2016/6/22/taxpayers-lose-virtually-all-charitable-contributions-for-failure-to-meet-strict-substantiation-rules-in-the-law-and-regulations); [GiftLaw ID=416 snippet](https://plannedgiving.mssu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=416); [onecle p.6](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2007/stamoulis.sum.wpd06.html)
- IRS Form 8283 instructions tell donors, for the "method used to determine FMV" column, to enter e.g. "Appraisal," "Thrift shop value" (for clothing or household items), "Catalog" (stamp/coin collections), or "Comparable sales" — i.e., "Thrift shop value" is an IRS-sanctioned method label. — [Instructions for Form 8283](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8283)
- Pub 561: "Valuation of items of clothing does not lend itself to fixed formulas or methods"; price guides "must be the current edition at the contribution date and should be supported by other evidence"; replacement cost "must" have "a reasonable relationship" to FMV; "The sales prices of properties similar to the donated property are often important in determining the FMV." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Nolo (practitioner): describes the Salvation Army and Goodwill guides as reference points and says "it's hard to imagine that the IRS would complain if a donor used one of these guides"; also suggests thrift-store and online-auction prices as comparables: "Prices obtained in the past for the sale of similar items are always a good indicator of value." — [search summary of Nolo](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/how-value-noncash-charitable-contributions.html)
- CPA Practice Advisor (on Bass): "the IRS doesn't specify amounts so you can rely on guidelines provided by organizations like Goodwill and the Salvation Army." — [CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2023/05/16/sizing-up-a-charitable-deduction-for-clothing-when-is-an-appraisal-required/79750/)
- Chronicle of Philanthropy (1998 IRS-guidance piece): a car's "Blue Book" value of $1,600 was adjusted to $750 after dealers were consulted, "showing that price guides are only a starting point." — [Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/opinion/what-the-irs-says-about-how-taxpayers-should-value-gifts-of-property/)

### Inferences
- The absence of any opinion attacking guide values is weak positive evidence: the IRS appears to concede small per-item thrift values (allowing $250–$1,500 even with no records) and litigates the substantiation defects instead. A guide whose values are anchored to observed thrift/consignment sale prices, with the comparables stored per value, would answer the Stamoulis and Copeland critiques (unmatched comparables, no resale evidence).
- Because Form 8283 itself offers "Thrift shop value" as a method, a dataset that documents how its thrift values were observed (store, date, region, condition) gives the donor an entry the IRS form anticipates and a record the regs require ("the method used in determining the fair market value").

### Gaps
- No case found in which a court affirmatively accepted an ItsDeductible, Goodwill-guide, or Salvation Army-guide value; also none rejecting such values as too high on the merits. The record is silent rather than hostile.
- No federal case found addressing eBay "sold" listings as comparables for clothing; the only online-comparable discussion is Copeland (state court, Facebook Marketplace).
- Could not confirm from primary text how the Ohde court characterized ItsDeductible beyond the practitioner summary.

## Key Question 3: Substantiation failures that most commonly sink these claims

### Takeaway
The recurring fatal defects, in rough order of frequency, are: receipts/CWAs that do not describe the items (Besaw, Kunkel, Ohde, Grainger, Roberts, Gaines); no contemporaneous itemized list (reconstructed spreadsheets in Kunkel, Ohde, Besaw get no weight); no acquisition date/manner and cost basis for the >$500 tier (Kunkel, Ohde, Gibson); no qualified appraisal once clothing or furniture aggregates above $5,000 (Smith, Grainger, Bass, Ohde, Gibson); Form 8283 not signed by donee/appraiser (Grainger, Ohde); and no evidence of "good used condition or better" under §170(f)(16) (Kunkel, Smith).

### Cited Findings
- Receipt without item description = no deduction even where the court believes the gift happened: Besaw ("none of the receipts ... included any descriptions of the donated items; therefore, petitioner is not entitled to deduct noncash charitable contributions"). — [Opinion PDF](https://bradfordtaxinstitute.com/Endnotes/TC_Summ_Op_2025-7.pdf)
- Goodwill receipts stating only "clothing" without the specific items or number donated per visit are not valid CWAs (Grainger); category-only receipts (clothing, shoes, media, furniture, household) with no descriptions, counts or condition (Ohde); "4 bags of clothes" (Roberts). — [The Tax Adviser 2018](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2018/oct/inflated-charitable-contribution-deduction-denied.html); [CPA Practice Advisor 2017](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2017/07/25/tax-court-denies-deduction-for-donation-of-20000-household-items/26389/); [TaxProf Blog](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/?p=7997)
- Doorknob hangers ("Thank you for your contribution") and unattended drop-offs: no receipt, no CWA; the court put the burden on the donor to obtain documentation even for unattended sites (Kunkel). — [Church Law & Tax](https://www.churchlawandtax.com/legal-developments/married-couples-37315-charitable-contribution-deduction-disallowed/); [Patch](https://patch.com/south-carolina/mountpleasant-sc/irs-charitable-deduction-audit)
- Receipts signed before the property was delivered were questioned as not being an "acknowledgment" (Smith). — [search summary of GiftLaw Pro ID=363](https://plannedgiving.mssu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=363)
- Reconstructed lists prepared during audit or after filing get no weight: Kunkel (audit spreadsheet), Ohde (ItsDeductible spreadsheet "not prepared at the time of the gifts"; post-trial value spreadsheet), Besaw ("Reconstructed from Form 8283" sent to IRS in 2022), Grainger (self-prepared spreadsheet "no evidentiary weight"). — [Church Law & Tax](https://www.churchlawandtax.com/legal-developments/married-couples-37315-charitable-contribution-deduction-disallowed/); [CPA Practice Advisor 2017](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2017/07/25/tax-court-denies-deduction-for-donation-of-20000-household-items/26389/); [Opinion PDF](https://bradfordtaxinstitute.com/Endnotes/TC_Summ_Op_2025-7.pdf); [The Tax Adviser 2018](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2018/oct/inflated-charitable-contribution-deduction-denied.html)
- Missing cost basis / acquisition data for the >$500 tier: Kunkel (no records of acquisition dates, manner, cost basis), Ohde (no cost basis on spreadsheet), Gibson ("the law requires detailed substantiation of the basis of the property"; "gift from a family member" with vague testimony not enough), RERI (basis blank on Form 8283 forfeited $33M). — [Church Law & Tax](https://www.churchlawandtax.com/legal-developments/married-couples-37315-charitable-contribution-deduction-disallowed/); [Current Federal Tax Developments 2026](https://www.currentfederaltaxdevelopments.com/blog/2026/1/6/strict-adherence-to-substantiation-requirements-an-analysis-of-gibson-v-commissioner); [The Tax Adviser 2019](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2019/mar/potential-pitfalls-charitable-contribution-substantiation-reporting/)
- No qualified appraisal when similar items exceed $5,000: Smith, Grainger, Bass, Ohde; non-qualified appraiser (Gibson). — [IRS CCA 202020002 items 49, 54](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-wd/202020002.pdf); [J.K. Lasser](https://www.jklasser.com/news/when-an-appraisal-for-donations-of-used-clothing-is-required/); [Current Federal Tax Developments 2026](https://www.currentfederaltaxdevelopments.com/blog/2026/1/6/strict-adherence-to-substantiation-requirements-an-analysis-of-gibson-v-commissioner)
- Form 8283 defects: not executed by a Goodwill official (Grainger); forms for groups over $5,000 not signed by donee or appraiser (Ohde); "Appraisal" listed as method with no appraisal attached (Gibson); self-prepared substitute Form 8283 (Roberts); values and dates omitted (Besaw). — [The Tax Adviser 2018](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2018/oct/inflated-charitable-contribution-deduction-denied.html); [The Tax Adviser 2019](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2019/mar/potential-pitfalls-charitable-contribution-substantiation-reporting/); [TaxProf Blog](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/?p=7997); [Opinion PDF](https://bradfordtaxinstitute.com/Endnotes/TC_Summ_Op_2025-7.pdf)
- No credible evidence items were "in good used condition or better" (§170(f)(16)(C)): Kunkel, Smith (per IRS Chief Counsel digest). — [IRS CCA 202020002](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-wd/202020002.pdf)
- No method of valuation stated (Gaines: "no method for determining the value"); no specific count of items (Payne). — [GiftLaw snippets](https://plannedgiving.mssu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=435)
- Photos: no case found where absence of photos was itself the ground of denial; practitioner guidance (Tax Adviser 2019; commenters on Roberts; Washington Times 2006 quoting a tax director on the 2006 rule) recommends photographing items and attaching an itemized list with values to the donee receipt. — [The Tax Adviser 2019](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2019/mar/potential-pitfalls-charitable-contribution-substantiation-reporting/); [TaxProf Blog](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/?p=7997); [search summary of Washington Times 2006](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2006/aug/18/20060818-113944-8022r/)
- Litigation statistics: TAS reviewed 29 charitable-deduction cases decided June 2017–May 2018; IRS prevailed in 24, taxpayers in 4, one split; 10 of 29 taxpayers were pro se and "the IRS prevailed fully in all 10"; substantiation was the issue in 18 cases, valuation in 4. — [TAS 2018 Annual Report, MLI #8 (PDF, text extracted locally)](https://www.taxpayeradvocate.irs.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/ARC18_Volume1_MLI_08_CharitableDeductions.pdf)
- June 2019–May 2020: 14 cases; taxpayer prevailed in 1, IRS in 12, one split; 3 pro se; substantiation at issue in 6; during the same period taxpayers petitioned the Tax Court in 401 cases where charitable contributions were an issue in examination. — [TAS 2020 Annual Report, MLI #9 (PDF, text extracted locally)](https://www.taxpayeradvocate.irs.gov/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/ARC20_MLI_09_CharitableDeductions.pdf)
- TIGTA Report 2013-40-009 (Dec. 2012) estimated $3.8 billion of potentially erroneous noncash deductions for TY2010 and a 74% error rate for contributions of $5,000–$500,000, many involving incorrectly completed or missing Forms 8283; the IRS agreed to flag returns claiming >$500 noncash without Form 8283. — [Journal of Accountancy, May 2013](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/issues/2013/may/noncash-charitable-contributions/)

### Inferences
- The product-level checklist a defensible app must enforce, derived from the above: (1) per-visit receipt from the charity that names the charity, date, location, and describes the items (type + count + condition) — generic "bags of clothes" fails; (2) for any single-visit donation of $250+, a CWA that also states no goods or services were received; (3) for annual noncash > $500, a record of how/when each item was acquired and its cost basis (or a documented basis estimate, since courts have rejected "it was a gift" without more); (4) a running per-category (similar-items) total with a hard warning at $5,000 that a qualified appraisal and Form 8283 Section B are required; (5) list created at or before drop-off, timestamped, not reconstructed; (6) per-item condition rating that is not uniformly "high."
- Because the IRS routinely concedes a few hundred dollars even on no records (Ohde $250, Haag $250, Payne $1,500), an app that reliably produces a compliant record for the typical $500–$4,999 donor is where the practical value lies; above $5,000/category the dataset cannot substitute for an appraisal and should say so.

### Gaps
- No empirical IRS data found on how often clothing/household deductions are examined or adjusted by amount; TIGTA figures are for all noncash >$5,000.
- Could not retrieve TAS 2019 and 2021+ MLI chapters' case tables to count clothing-specific cases.

## Key Question 4: What courts and the IRS say the correct market is for used clothing (thrift vs. retail vs. online resale) — quoted language

### Takeaway
The IRS's published position is explicit — "A good measure of value might be the price that buyers of these used items actually pay in consignment or thrift shops" — and courts have rejected retail price tags (Grainger), replacement cost new and unmatched online listings (Copeland), while treating the charity's actual resale prices as the best evidence (Stamoulis); no court has endorsed a specific percentage-of-cost rule, and Pub 526/561 say formulas are not acceptable.

### Cited Findings
- Form 8283 instructions: "FMV is the price a willing, knowledgeable buyer would pay a willing, knowledgeable seller when neither has to buy or sell." For used items: "A good measure of value might be the price that buyers of these used items actually pay in consignment or thrift shops." — [Instructions for Form 8283](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8283)
- Pub 561: "FMV is the price that property would sell for on the open market." "Used clothing and other personal items are usually worth far less than the price you paid for them." "Valuation of items of clothing does not lend itself to fixed formulas or methods." The price buyers actually pay "in used clothing stores, such as consignment or thrift shops," indicates value. "The FMV of used household items is usually much lower than the price paid when new." "Sentimental personal value has no effect on FMV." "You may consider only the facts known at the time of the gift." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Pub 526 (per summary of the "Determining FMV" section): use the price buyers actually pay for similar used items at consignment or thrift shops; example of a coat bought for $300 whose thrift counterparts sell for $50, so FMV is $50; for household items, formulas such as a percentage of replacement cost are not acceptable; support valuation with photos, receipts or similar evidence but do not attach them to the return. — [IRS Pub 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- The Tax Adviser (2016): "Pricing formulas based on a percentage of original cost are not acceptable under IRS Publication 526. For used clothing, the relevant value is what buyers actually pay at used-clothing retailers such as consignment or thrift shops." — [The Tax Adviser, July 2016](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2016/jul/valuing-and-substantiating-charitable-contributions.html)
- IRS news release IR-2009-114 / "Eight Essentials" page: "The value of used clothing or household goods is usually far less than what you paid for the property. A good indication of the value of used clothing is the price that a buyer would pay in used clothing stores, such as consignment or thrift stores." — [search summary of IRS IR-09-114 and irs.gov/node/7430](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-news/ir-09-114.pdf)
- Grainger: "no rational buyer having knowledge of the relevant facts would have paid for these items a price higher than the price Talbots was then charging"; retail list price "does not qualify as a willing buyer and willing seller amount under Reg. 1.170A-1(c)(2)." — [GiftLaw Pro / Lerch Early summaries](https://www.lerchearly.com/news/donation-of-discounted-goods-cannot-get-list-price-deduction-grainger-tcm-2018-117/)
- Stamoulis: used clothing "will be worth far less than their original purchase price" immediately after purchase; the thrift store's resale prices "would certainly be relevant and persuasive evidence"; FMV determination "is, at best, an inexact science." — [onecle p.10](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2007/stamoulis.sum.wpd10.html); [p.11](https://law.onecle.com/tax/2007/stamoulis.sum.wpd11.html)
- Copeland (Or. Tax Ct. 2025, applying Treas. Reg. FMV definition): "replacement cost new is not a reliable measure of FMV" for used clothing and household goods; "[c]ourts have consistently recognized that, in general, comparable sales constitute the best evidence of market value"; replacement cost counts "only" if it matches "what a willing and knowledgeable buyer would actually pay in the marketplace." — [vLex](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/copeland-v-dep-t-1091465844)
- Haag (quoting reg): FMV is "the price at which the property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller." — [Current Federal Tax Developments 2016](https://www.currentfederaltaxdevelopments.com/blog/2016/6/22/taxpayers-lose-virtually-all-charitable-contributions-for-failure-to-meet-strict-substantiation-rules-in-the-law-and-regulations)
- Pub 561 correction notice (Rev. Mar. 2023): clothing must be in good used condition or better; an item not in that condition claimed at more than $500 requires a qualified appraisal and completed Form 8283 (page now marked historical). — [IRS correction notice](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/correction-of-publication-561-rev-mar-2023-regarding-used-clothing)
- IRM 4.48.3 (Tangible Personal Property Valuation Guidelines, rev. 9/22/2020) governs IRS valuation staff for vehicles, antiques, art, collectibles, furniture, machinery; it does not define FMV or address used clothing/household goods and does not discuss retail vs. wholesale vs. auction markets as such. — [IRM 4.48.3](https://www.irs.gov/irm/part4/irm_04-048-003)

### Inferences
- The legally relevant market is the market in which the *donated item in its used condition* actually trades to the public — for ordinary clothing and housewares that is the thrift/consignment shelf, which is also where the donee will sell it. Online resale (eBay sold, Poshmark) is a plausible comparable market for branded/designer items, but Copeland shows courts will reject online comparables that are not matched item-for-item; the dataset should store the comparable's identity, date, condition and price, not just a derived number.
- A percentage-of-cost rule of thumb (e.g., the 20% figure from the Stamoulis press coverage) is useful only as a sanity check; publishing it as a method would contradict Pub 526/561.

### Gaps
- No federal opinion found that explicitly chooses between "thrift" and "online resale" markets for clothing; the retail-vs-used distinction is well established but the thrift-vs-online question is undecided.
- Pub 526's exact current wording on formulas was summarized by the fetch tool, not quoted verbatim; confirm against the live publication before quoting.

## Key Question 5: The "similar items" aggregation rule for the $5,000 qualified-appraisal threshold — how courts group items

### Takeaway
Courts and the IRS treat all clothing as one "similar items" group (Grainger, Bass) and have said furniture and clothing would each be aggregated (Smith); the aggregation is across all donees and all visits in the year, so splitting donations into sub-$250 trips (Bass, Kunkel) does nothing; the governing text defines similar items as "property of the same generic category or type" with clothing, books, jewelry and furniture among the IRS's listed examples.

### Cited Findings
- Reg. 1.170A-13(c)(7)(iii) / Form 8283 instructions: "Similar items of property are items of the same general category or type, such as coin collections, paintings, books, clothing, jewelry, nonpublicly traded stock, land, or buildings." Example: "You claimed a deduction of $6,000 for a collection of six rare books ($1,000 each)" must be reported in Section B. "Use Section A to report donations of property for which you claimed a deduction of $5,000 or less"; "Use Section B ... more than $5,000." — [Instructions for Form 8283](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8283)
- Pub 561: "The phrase 'similar items' means property of the same generic category or type," listing stamps, coins, fine art, books, clothing, etc.; "if the claimed deduction for an item or group of similar items of donated property is more than $5,000," a qualified appraisal and Form 8283 Section B are generally required; similar items given to different donees must be added together (example of books given to different schools); one Form 8283 per donee for similar items; one qualified appraisal may cover "a group of similar items of property contributed in the same tax year" and the appraiser may give a group description for selected items worth $100 or less in total. — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Pub 526: to determine whether the deduction is $500 or more, combine deductions claimed for all similar items donated to any qualified organization during the year. — [IRS Pub 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- Grainger: "all donated items were clothing, so they were treated as 'similar items of property' (Regs. Sec. 1.170A-13(c)(7)(iii)) and aggregated. The combined claim exceeded $5,000, triggering the qualified appraisal and appraisal summary requirements." — [The Tax Adviser 2018](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2018/oct/inflated-charitable-contribution-deduction-denied.html)
- Bass: taxpayer argued no single item exceeded $5,000; court held similar items must be aggregated "whether donated to one charity or several," so clothing of $13,852 (Goodwill) + $11,594 (Salvation Army) needed a qualified appraisal; 173 sub-$250 trips did not help. — [J.K. Lasser](https://www.jklasser.com/news/when-an-appraisal-for-donations-of-used-clothing-is-required/); [CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2023/05/16/sizing-up-a-charitable-deduction-for-clothing-when-is-an-appraisal-required/79750/)
- Smith: "The court also suggested that all furniture and clothing items would be aggregated for this purpose"; IRS digest: "claimed deduction in excess of $5000 without obtaining a qualified appraisal." — [search summary of GiftLaw Pro ID=363](https://plannedgiving.mssu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=363); [IRS CCA 202020002](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-wd/202020002.pdf)
- Kunkel: court applied the rule that similar items must be aggregated for the $500 and $5,000 thresholds (per secondary summaries); the claimed clothing alone was $21,920 across donees. — [GiftLaw Pro ID=376 snippet](https://legacy.jwu.edu/giftlawpro/glawpro_cases.jsp?ID=376); [Church Law & Tax](https://www.churchlawandtax.com/legal-developments/married-couples-37315-charitable-contribution-deduction-disallowed/)
- Ohde: Forms 8283 "for groups over $5,000" lacked donee/appraiser signatures, implying the IRS/court grouped the 20,000 items into several >$5,000 similar-item groups (clothing, furniture, books, etc.). — [The Tax Adviser 2019](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2019/mar/potential-pitfalls-charitable-contribution-substantiation-reporting/)
- Virginia Tax Commissioner rulings (2025) apply the same federal rule: a clothing donation aggregated above $5,000 required a qualified appraisal the couple lacked. — [search summary of VA P.D. 25-118 / 25-48](https://www.tax.virginia.gov/laws-rules-decisions/rulings-tax-commissioner/25-118)
- A single clothing or household item not in good used condition can be deducted only if claimed above $500 with a qualified appraisal and Form 8283 Section B (§170(f)(16)); household items are furniture, furnishings, electronics, appliances, linens and similar items, excluding food, art, antiques, jewelry, gems and collections. — [Instructions for Form 8283](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8283); [IRS IR-2016-50](https://irs.gov/pub/irs-news/IR-16-050.pdf); [The Tax Adviser 2016](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2016/jul/valuing-and-substantiating-charitable-contributions.html)

### Inferences
- No opinion was found drawing finer lines (e.g., men's vs. women's clothing, or furniture vs. "household items"); the safe product rule is to aggregate at the generic-category level the IRS lists (clothing; books; furniture; household items/housewares; electronics; toys; jewelry) and to treat uncertainty conservatively (warn earlier, e.g., at $4,000).
- Because one appraisal can cover a group and may use a group description for items worth $100 or less in total, an itemized export from the app is exactly the document a qualified appraiser needs; the dataset could ship an "appraiser packet" format.

### Gaps
- No court opinion text retrieved that walks through category-by-category grouping (e.g., whether "household items" and "furniture" are one group or two); the regulation's non-exhaustive list leaves that open.

## Key Question 6: IRS audit practice — what examiners ask for; IRM/ATG sections; IRS tax tips and news releases

### Takeaway
No IRM section or Audit Technique Guide specific to examining clothing/household donations was found (IRM 4.48.3 covers IRS valuation staff for art/vehicles/furniture and never mentions clothing; IRM 4.10.7 has nothing on charitable contributions); the practical examiner standard is visible in the Besaw notice of deficiency ("name and address of the qualifying organization(s) ... a list of what was donated ... the fair market value of each item on the date of contribution") and in Pub 526's "Records to keep" tiers, which track the regulation examiners apply.

### Cited Findings
- IRS examiner's stated requirement (Besaw NOD, 2022): "To be allowed a deduction for property as a contribution, you must show (a) the name and address of the qualifying organization(s), (b) provide a list of what was donated, and (c) show the fair market value of each item on the date of contribution." — [Opinion PDF](https://bradfordtaxinstitute.com/Endnotes/TC_Summ_Op_2025-7.pdf)
- Pub 526 "Records To Keep" (noncash): under $250 — receipt with charity name/address, date and location, reasonably detailed description; if a receipt is impractical, reliable written records including FMV and how determined and, for clothing/household items, their condition. $250–$500 — CWA describing the property (not value) and stating whether goods/services were received. Over $500–$5,000 — Form 8283 Section A plus the CWA; records of FMV and valuation method, condition, how and approximately when acquired, cost or other basis. Over $5,000 — CWA, qualified appraisal, Form 8283 Section B with donee signature and appraiser declaration. — [IRS Pub 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- IRS news release IR-2016-50 ("Tax Time Guide: Good Records Key to Claiming Gifts to Charity," Mar. 28, 2016): donors must get a written acknowledgment for all contributions of $250 or more; "For donations of property, the acknowledgement must include, among other things, a description of the items contributed"; acknowledgements must be in hand before filing; "If the amount of the deduction for all noncash contributions is over $500, a properly-completed Form 8283 is required"; "Clothing and household items donated to charity generally must be in good used condition or better to be tax-deductible. A clothing or household item for which a taxpayer claims a deduction of over $500 does not have to meet this standard if the taxpayer includes a qualified appraisal of the item with the return." — [IRS IR-2016-50 (PDF, text extracted locally)](https://irs.gov/pub/irs-news/IR-16-050.pdf)
- IRS "Deducting Charitable Contributions: Eight Essentials" and IR-2009-114: "Clothing and household items must generally be in good used condition or better to be deductible"; get a receipt including the charity's name, date, and description of items; value of used clothing is what a buyer would pay in consignment or thrift stores. — [IRS Eight Essentials](https://www.irs.gov/node/7430); [IR-2009-114](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-news/ir-09-114.pdf)
- IRS Chief Counsel maintains an internal digest, "Select Charitable Contribution Cases 2012 Forward" (CCA 202020002, released 5/15/2020; earlier version dated 1/14/2020), circulated from CC:ITA to SB/SE, which summarizes Smith and Kunkel as the clothing/household precedents examiners can rely on. — [CCA 202020002](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-wd/202020002.pdf); [Jan. 2020 version](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-counsel/20200114-partial-charitable-contribution-case-summaries.pdf)
- IRM 4.48.3 (Tangible Personal Property Valuation Guidelines): applies to IRS employees who provide or review valuations; scope is vehicles, antiques, silver, artwork, collectibles, furniture, machinery and equipment; requires reports to explain methodology, market conditions near the valuation date, acquisition cost, condition and comparable/auction sales; does not mention used clothing or household goods. — [IRM 4.48.3](https://www.irs.gov/irm/part4/irm_04-048-003)
- IRM 4.10.7 (Issue Resolution) contains no guidance on charitable contributions, Form 8283 or valuation of donated property. — [IRM 4.10.7](https://www.irs.gov/irm/part4/irm_04-010-007)
- IRS Art Appraisal Services reviews Form 8283 appraisals for other IRS offices; GAO-12-608 reported IRS audit selection "does not specifically target noncash contributions" supported by appraisals. — [search summary of irs.gov AAS page and GAO-12-608](https://www.gao.gov/products/GAO-12-608)
- TIGTA: IRS agreed to use an indicator to route returns claiming >$500 noncash without a Form 8283 to Examination; later TIGTA found 18% of errors on returns claiming >$5,000 noncash involved missing documents and none had been examined. — [search summary of Tax Notes TIGTA coverage](https://www.taxnotes.com/lr/resolve/the-exempt-organization-tax-review/tigta-says-more-education-needed-on-charitable-contributions/tcby); [Journal of Accountancy 2013](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/issues/2013/may/noncash-charitable-contributions/)
- Tax attorney (Klasing) on what examiners actually do: cases are built "from documents rather than testimony"; examiners "typically request donation receipts, appraisals, photographs, purchase records, and the attachments required with the return, then compare that package to the substantiation rules"; for noncash the IRS "expects support for what was donated, when, its condition where required, and how the value was determined"; a bank statement alone does not satisfy the $250 rule; "missing documents generally cannot be fixed later"; audit risk rises with "aggressive noncash values or unusually large gifts relative to income." — [Klasing & Associates](https://klasing-associates.com/charitable-contribution-audits-substantiation-pitfalls/)
- A Goodwill-donation audit described by a practitioner: taxpayers "did not produce any documentary evidence as to which items were donated to which charity" and produced an audit-time spreadsheet "with estimated amounts for the fair market value"; reconstructed records "may not cure a documentation gap"; taxpayers wrongly "assume the charity's receipt satisfies the IRS documentation requirements." — [search summary of getirshelp.com and Bradford](https://getirshelp.com/blog/?p=4306)
- Penalty exposure relevant to overvaluation: 20% penalty if claimed value is 150% or more of the correct amount and the resulting underpayment exceeds $5,000; 40% if 200% or more; accuracy penalties were sustained in Kunkel, Ohde, Smith and Bass, waived in Stamoulis (optimistic but not negligent) and conceded in Besaw. — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561); case sources above
- Goodwill and the Salvation Army both state the donor assigns the value and their receipts generally carry no dollar amounts; Goodwill notes items must be in "good used condition or better." — [Goodwill WA digital receipt page](https://goodwillwa.org/donate/taxreceipt/); [Salvation Army valuation guide](https://satruck.org/Home/DonationValueGuide)

### Inferences
- Examiners are applying Reg. 1.170A-13(b) and Pub 526 tiers mechanically; there is no evidence they run item-level valuation challenges on ordinary clothing. The audit outcome hinges on whether the donor can hand over (a) charity receipts that describe the items, (b) a contemporaneous itemized list with condition and method, (c) acquisition/basis data, and (d) an appraisal above $5,000 per category.
- Because no ATG exists, the IRS's own Form 8283 instructions ("Thrift shop value") and Pub 561/526 are the best statements of what an examiner would accept as a method; a dataset that cites them and documents its comparables is aligned with the examiner's framework.

### Gaps
- No IRM section found that instructs field examiners on clothing/household-goods audits (searched IRM Part 4 generally; 4.10.7 and 4.48.3 fetched; others not located by search). An IRS Audit Technique Guide on individual charitable contributions could not be found; it may not exist.
- No IRS Tax Tip located after 2016 specifically on valuing donated goods; recent IRS pages (Topic 506, Pub 526/561) restate the same rules.
- No data on IRS correspondence-exam document request lists (Form 886-A / IDR templates) for this issue.

## Key Question 7: Practitioner guidance (CPAs, EAs, tax attorneys) on documenting and valuing these donations, and what they say about valuation guides

### Takeaway
Practitioner guidance is uniform: value at thrift/consignment prices, never a percentage of cost or replacement cost; keep an itemized contemporaneous list with condition and method, photos, and a per-visit charity receipt that describes the items; add up similar items across all charities and get a qualified appraisal once a category exceeds $5,000; guides from Goodwill/Salvation Army/ItsDeductible are treated as acceptable starting points that need support, not as authority.

### Cited Findings
- The Tax Adviser (AICPA, 2016): taxpayer bears the burden of establishing FMV; percentage-of-cost formulas not acceptable; thrift/consignment prices are the relevant market; items in less than "good" condition are "fully disallowed"; "The IRS may later disallow deductions for low-value items such as underwear and socks, regardless of condition"; strict rather than substantial compliance advised. — [The Tax Adviser, July 2016](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2016/jul/valuing-and-substantiating-charitable-contributions.html)
- The Tax Adviser (2019): CWA must include donee name, date, amount of cash, "description (but not value) of non-cash property," goods-or-services statement; "donation is deductible to the full extent of the law" is insufficient language; for clothing and household items the suggested practice is "to photograph items and attach an itemized list with values to the donee receipt"; a qualified appraisal must exist before filing — if discovered late, "file without the deduction and amend later"; carryover-year returns must carry the same substantiation (Regs. 1.170A-16(f)(3)). — [The Tax Adviser, Mar. 2019](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2019/mar/potential-pitfalls-charitable-contribution-substantiation-reporting/)
- Journal of Accountancy (2013): Form 8283 required above $500; taxpayers "must group similar items and aggregate their values when applying the reporting threshold"; 74% error rate on $5,000–$500,000 contributions per TIGTA. — [Journal of Accountancy, May 2013](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/issues/2013/may/noncash-charitable-contributions/)
- CPA Practice Advisor (2023): "Adhere strictly to the substantiation rules"; document all contributions and obtain detailed receipts; rely on Goodwill/Salvation Army guidelines because the IRS does not specify amounts. — [CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2023/05/16/sizing-up-a-charitable-deduction-for-clothing-when-is-an-appraisal-required/79750/)
- J.K. Lasser: add up similar items across all charities for the year; "A group of small donations can cross the $5,000 threshold"; "A charity receipt with a blank valuation section is not a substitute for a qualified appraisal." — [J.K. Lasser](https://www.jklasser.com/news/when-an-appraisal-for-donations-of-used-clothing-is-required/)
- Nolo: Salvation Army and Goodwill guides are reference points; "it's hard to imagine that the IRS would complain if a donor used one of these guides"; the Salvation Army "provides a blank receipt and does not appraise"; past sale prices of similar items (thrift stores, online auctions) are "always a good indicator of value"; single item ≥$500 exception to the condition rule requires an appraisal. — [search summary of Nolo](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/how-value-noncash-charitable-contributions.html)
- Tax attorney (Klasing): keep documents matching "the exact substantiation requirements"; keep federal and state filings consistent; involve counsel early for large noncash deductions or missing acknowledgments. — [Klasing & Associates](https://klasing-associates.com/charitable-contribution-audits-substantiation-pitfalls/)
- Holland & Knight on Besaw: "there are no second chances to fix documentation problems after the fact"; the documentation duty applies even when the donations are undisputed. — [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/08/taxpayer-loses-charitable-deduction-due-to-lack-of-documentation)
- Church Law & Tax / Marcum-type commentary: a written record for clothing and household items needs a description of their condition; the court "did not question that he made the contributions," underscoring that real donations can be disallowed. — [search summary (Church Law & Tax; Marcum)](https://www.churchlawandtax.com/legal-developments/married-couples-37315-charitable-contribution-deduction-disallowed/)
- Practitioner reaction to the 2006 "good used condition" rule (Pension Protection Act): the statute does not define "good used condition"; a tax director advised donors to "keep even more pristine records of these donations and take pictures of items before donating them." — [search summary of Washington Times, Aug. 18, 2006](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2006/aug/18/20060818-113944-8022r/)
- Chronicle of Philanthropy (1998, summarizing IRS guidance): donors, not charities, determine FMV; four factors — original cost or the charity's resale price, sales of comparable items, replacement cost, expert opinions; worn or outdated items may have "little or no market value." — [Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/opinion/what-the-irs-says-about-how-taxpayers-should-value-gifts-of-property/)
- Commenters on the Roberts case (TaxProf Blog) suggested photographing items, preparing typed itemized lists, and having charity staff sign them; they viewed ~$64/item as implausibly high. — [TaxProf Blog](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/?p=7997)
- Bradford Tax Institute runs paywalled articles on Besaw ("Brutal IRS Trap Wipes Out Goodwill Clothing Deductions") and Bass ("Why Did Duncan Bass Make 172 Trips..."), framing the rules as "rigid and unforgiving" and covering qualifying charities, eligible items, valuation, records, reporting, and avoiding penalties. — [Bradford (Besaw)](https://bradfordtaxinstitute.com/Content/Brutal-IRS-Trap-Wipes-Out-Goodwill-Clothing-Deductions.aspx); [Bradford (Bass)](https://blog.bradfordtaxinstitute.com/why-did-duncan-bass-make-172-trips-to-goodwill-and-the-salvation-army)

### Inferences
- The practitioner consensus maps directly onto product requirements: (1) item-level list with quantity, description, condition and method ("Thrift shop value"), (2) photo capture, (3) receipt that references the list, (4) basis/acquisition capture, (5) similar-items running total with appraisal gate, (6) export in Form 8283 Section A column order. None of the practitioner sources objects to using a published guide; several explicitly endorse it as the starting point.
- The strongest credibility differentiator for DonationValues.org versus existing guides would be publishing the comparable-sales evidence behind each value (store, region, date, condition), since Pub 561 says guides "should be supported by other evidence" and Stamoulis/Copeland both turned on missing or unmatched comparables.

### Gaps
- Could not read Forbes (Peter Reilly), Procedurally Taxing, Kiplinger, Schneider Downs, Marcum/CBIZ or Bradford full texts (paywalls, 403/404, redirects); their positions are inferred from search snippets or not included.
- No practitioner source quantified what per-item values the IRS has accepted in exam; the "average for similarly situated taxpayers" benchmark used in Stamoulis was not explained in the retrieved pages (it presumably came from IRS statistics of income data, but the opinion pages read do not say).

## Q1. The regulatory FMV definition (Treas. Reg. §1.170A-1(c)(2)) and the "market in which such item is most commonly sold to the public" language

### Takeaway
§1.170A-1(c)(2) supplies the willing-buyer/willing-seller definition, but the "market in which such item is most commonly sold to the public" sentence is NOT in §1.170A-1; it comes from the estate-tax valuation regulation, §20.2031-1(b) (mirrored in the gift-tax reg §25.2512-1). For used clothing and household goods, the IRS applies the same willing-buyer standard and tells donors the relevant market is where used goods actually trade (consignment/thrift shops), not original retail.

### Cited Findings
- Verbatim, §1.170A-1(c)(1): "If a charitable contribution is made in property other than money, the amount of the contribution is the fair market value of the property at the time of the contribution reduced as provided in section 170(e)(1) and paragraph (a) of § 1.170A-4, or section 170(e)(3) and paragraph (c) of § 1.170A-4A." — [26 CFR 1.170A-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.170A-1)
- Verbatim, §1.170A-1(c)(2): "The fair market value is the price at which the property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under any compulsion to buy or sell and both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts. If the contribution is made in property of a type which the taxpayer sells in the course of his business, the fair market value is the price which the taxpayer would have received if he had sold the contributed property in the usual market in which he customarily sells, at the time and place of the contribution and, in the case of a contribution of goods in quantity, in the quantity contributed. The usual market of a manufacturer or other producer consists of the wholesalers or other distributors to or through whom he customarily sells, but if he sells only at retail the usual market consists of his retail customers." — [26 CFR 1.170A-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.170A-1)
- Verbatim, §1.170A-1(c)(3): "If a donor makes a charitable contribution of property, such as stock in trade, at a time when he could not reasonably have been expected to realize its usual selling price, the value of the gift is not the usual selling price but is the amount for which the quantity of property contributed would have been sold by the donor at the time of the contribution." — [26 CFR 1.170A-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.170A-1)
- The phrase "market in which such item is most commonly sold to the public" does not appear in §1.170A-1 (confirmed by full-text check of the Cornell LII page). — [26 CFR 1.170A-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.170A-1)
- The phrase is in the estate-tax regulation §20.2031-1(b), which (per the LII text) provides: FMV is "the price at which the property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller," neither under compulsion and both with reasonable knowledge of relevant facts; FMV "is not to be determined by a forced sale price"; nor is it "to be determined by the sale price of the item in a market other than that in which such item is most commonly sold to the public, taking into account the location of the item wherever appropriate"; and for an item "generally obtained by the public in the retail market," FMV is "the price at which the item or a comparable item would be sold at retail." The reg's example: a decedent's automobile is valued at what a member of the public would pay for a similar vehicle, not what a used-car dealer would pay for it. — [26 CFR 20.2031-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/20.2031-1)
- The IRS restates the definition for donors as: "FMV is the price that property would sell for on the open market. It is the price that would be agreed on between a willing buyer and a willing seller, with neither being required to act, and both having reasonable knowledge of the relevant facts." (verbatim, Pub 561 (12/2025), "What Is Fair Market Value (FMV)?") — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Application to clothing, verbatim, Pub 561 Example 1 under the FMV definition: "If you give an item of used clothing that is in good used condition or better to the Salvation Army, the FMV would be the price that typical buyers actually pay for clothing of this age, condition, style, and use. Usually, such items are worth far less than what you paid for them." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Form 8283 instructions (12/2025) definition, verbatim: "FMV is the price a willing, knowledgeable buyer would pay a willing, knowledgeable seller when neither has to buy or sell." — [IRS i8283](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8283)
- Pub 561 (12/2025) on open-market requirement, verbatim ("Unusual Market Conditions"): "The sale price of the property itself in an arm's-length transaction in an open market is often the best evidence of its value. When you rely on sales of comparable property, the sales must have been made in an open market. If those sales were made in a market that was artificially supported or stimulated so as not to be truly representative, the prices at which the sales were made will not indicate the FMV. For example, liquidation sale prices usually do not indicate the FMV." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Pub 561 (12/2025) Example 1 under "Sales of Comparable Properties" (religious books bought from a promoter) holds that a non-dealer donor's FMV is the price at which "similar quantities of books were being sold to the general public by wholesale dealers at the time of the contribution," not the promoter's "retail" figure — i.e., the market in which the donor could actually sell. — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)

### Inferences
- A dataset that claims to represent FMV of used clothing/household goods should be anchored to the secondhand market in which such goods are "most commonly sold to the public" (thrift/consignment/resale), not to original retail or to replacement cost; §20.2031-1(b)'s "retail market" sentence, read with Pub 561's clothing example, points to the retail price of the *used* item in the used-goods market, not the new-goods retail price.
- The inventory language in §1.170A-1(c)(2)-(3) ("usual market," "quantity contributed") is not directly about individual donors, but Pub 561's examples extend the same logic: the relevant price is what the donor could actually realize in the market open to the donor at the time of the gift.
- Citing "§1.170A-1(c)(2)" for the "most commonly sold to the public" sentence would be an error that a reviewer could catch; DonationValues.org should cite §20.2031-1(b) (and §25.2512-1) for that sentence and §1.170A-1(c)(2) for the willing-buyer definition.

### Gaps
- I did not independently fetch §25.2512-1 (gift-tax reg); its wording is widely reported to parallel §20.2031-1(b) but was not verified here.
- No IRS income-tax regulation or ruling was found that explicitly imports the §20.2031-1(b) "most commonly sold to the public" sentence into §170 valuation; Pub 561 applies the concept by example only.

## Q2. What Publication 561 (12/2025) says about used clothing, household items, thrift-shop value, comparable sales, and valuation guides

### Takeaway
Pub 561 (12/2025) says clothing valuation "does not lend itself to fixed formulas or methods," that prices "buyers of used items actually pay in used clothing stores, such as consignment or thrift shops" are "an indication of the value," that household items are "usually much lower than the price paid when new," and that published price guides generally "are not always reliable indicators of FMV and should be supported by other evidence."

### Cited Findings
- Revision date: "Publication 561 (12/2025) … Revised: December 2025". — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Verbatim, "Used Clothing" section (complete): "Used clothing and other personal items are usually worth far less than the price you paid for them. Valuation of items of clothing does not lend itself to fixed formulas or methods. The price that buyers of used items actually pay in used clothing stores, such as consignment or thrift shops, is an indication of the value. You cannot take an income tax charitable contribution deduction for an item of clothing unless it is in good used condition or better. An item of clothing that is not in good used condition or better for which you claim an income tax charitable contribution deduction of more than $500 requires a qualified appraisal and a completed Form 8283. See Deduction over $500 for certain clothing or household items, later." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Verbatim, "Household Items" section (complete): "The FMV of used household items is usually much lower than the price paid when new. Household items include furniture, furnishings, electronics, appliances, linens, and similar items. Household items do not include paintings, antiques, objects of art, jewelry, gems, and collections like stamp and coin collections. Such used property may have little or no market value because it may be out of style. You cannot take an income tax charitable contribution deduction for household items unless they are in good used condition or better. The one exception to this is a household item that is not in good used condition or better for which you claim an income tax charitable contribution deduction of more than $500. In this case, you must obtain a qualified appraisal valuing the item and complete a Form 8283. See Deduction over $500 for certain clothing or household items, later. If the property is valuable because it is old or unique, see Art and Collectibles, later." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Verbatim, "Deduction over $500 for certain clothing or household items": "You must include with your return a qualified appraisal prepared by a qualified appraiser of any single item of clothing or any household item that is not in good used condition or better and for which you deduct more than $500. Attach the appraisal and Form 8283. See Household Items and Used Clothing, earlier. You must also obtain a CWA for this donation." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Verbatim, "Factors": "In making and supporting the valuation of property, all factors affecting value are relevant and must be considered. These include, but are not limited to: The cost or selling price of the item, Sales of comparable properties, Replacement cost, and Opinions of professional appraisers." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Verbatim, "Determining FMV": "Determining the value of donated property depends upon many factors. You should consider all the facts and circumstances connected with the property, including any recent transactions, in determining value. Value may also be based on desirability, use, condition, scarcity, and market demand for that property." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Verbatim, "Cost or Selling Price of the Donated Property": "The cost of the property to you or the actual selling price received by the qualified organization may be the best indication of its FMV. However, because conditions in the market change, the cost or selling price of property may have less weight if the property was not bought or sold at a time that is reasonably close to the date of contribution." The cost/selling price is a good indication if: "The purchase or sale took place close to the valuation date in an open market, The purchase or sale was at 'arm's-length,' The buyer and seller knew all relevant facts, The buyer and seller did not have to act, and The economic conditions in the open market did not change between the date of purchase or sale and the valuation date." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Verbatim, "Sales of Comparable Properties": "The sales prices of properties similar to the donated property are often important in determining the FMV. The weight to be given to each sale depends on the following. The degree of similarity between the property sold and the donated property. The time of the sale—whether it was close to the valuation date. The circumstances of the sale—whether it was at arm's-length with a knowledgeable buyer and seller, with neither having to act. Whether the comparable sale is from the same market area as the donated property. The conditions of the market in which the sale was made—whether unusually inflated or deflated." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Verbatim, "Selection of Comparable Sales": "Using sales of comparable property is an important method for determining the FMV of donated property. However, the amount of weight given to a sale depends on the degree of similarity between the comparable and the donated properties. The degree of similarity must be close enough so that this selling price would have been given consideration by reasonably well-informed buyers or sellers of the property." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Verbatim, "Replacement Cost": "The cost of buying, building, or manufacturing property similar to the donated item may be considered in determining FMV. However, there must be a reasonable relationship between the replacement cost and the FMV. … In most cases, there is no relationship between the replacement cost and the FMV." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Verbatim, Example 2 under the FMV definition (insurance appraisals): an insurance-replacement appraisal "does not reflect what a willing buyer and willing seller would pay for the jewelry on the date of the contribution to charity. It reflects only the replacement cost". — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- On price guides (in the Collectibles section), verbatim: "Publications available to help you determine the value of many kinds of collections include catalogs, dealers' price lists, and specialized hobby periodicals. When using one of these price guides, you must use the current edition at the date of contribution. … These sources are not always reliable indicators of FMV and should be supported by other evidence. For example, a dealer may sell an item for much less than is shown on a price list, particularly after the item has remained unsold for a long time. The price an item sold for in an auction may have been the result of a rigged sale. The appraiser must analyze the reference material and recognize and make adjustments for misleading entries." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- On vehicle guides (the only place Pub 561 discusses third-party pricing guides in depth), verbatim: "The prices are not 'official,' and these publications are not considered an appraisal of any specific donated property. But they do provide clues for making an appraisal and suggest relative prices for comparison with current sales and offerings in your area." and "You can also find pricing information about used cars on the Internet." and the rule that FMV of a vehicle is "an amount not in excess of the price listed in a used vehicle pricing guide for a private party sale, not the dealer retail value, of a similar vehicle." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Pub 561 (12/2025) does not mention eBay, online auctions, or any named clothing/household valuation guide (Salvation Army, Goodwill, ItsDeductible). The only "Internet" reference is for used-car pricing; the only "auction" references are to art sold at auction and to rigged auction sales. (Full-text grep of the 12/2025 HTML.) — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Pub 561 "Penalty" section, verbatim: "You may be liable for a penalty if you misstate the value or adjusted basis of donated property. 20% penalty. The penalty is 20% of the underpayment of tax related to the misstatement if: The value or adjusted basis claimed on the return is 150% or more of the correct amount, and You underpaid your tax by more than $5,000 because of the misstatement. 40% penalty. The penalty is 40%, rather than 20%, if: The value or adjusted basis claimed on the return is 200% or more of the correct amount, and You underpaid your tax by more than $5,000 because of the misstatement." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)

### Inferences
- The IRS's own evidentiary hierarchy for used goods is: (1) actual arm's-length sale price of the item itself or the charity's resale price; (2) comparable sales in the same market area close to the date; (3) price guides only as "clues," to be "supported by other evidence"; (4) replacement cost essentially never. A defensible dataset should therefore be built from observed thrift/consignment/resale transaction prices (comparable sales) and should disclose the date, market, and condition grade behind each value, so that it functions as "other evidence" rather than as a bare price list.
- Pub 561's vehicle rule (use the private-party figure, not dealer retail, and never exceed the guide) is the closest IRS analogue for how a price guide for used personal property should be used: as a ceiling tied to the market the donor could actually sell into.

### Gaps
- Pub 561 does not define "good used condition" and does not give any numeric or percentage-of-cost rule for clothing or household items; the IRS has never published per-item values.

## Q3. What Publication 526 (2025) says about clothing/household items, the "good used condition or better" rule (IRC §170(f)(16)), the $500 single-item exception, and the definition of "household item"

### Takeaway
Pub 526 (2025) restates §170(f)(16): no deduction for clothing or household items unless in "good used condition or better," with the sole exception being a single item deducted at more than $500 and supported by a qualified appraisal and Form 8283 Section B. "Household items" are furniture/furnishings, electronics, appliances, linens and similar items, and exclude food, art/antiques, jewelry/gems, and collections. Pub 526 also states that for household items, "formulas (such as using a percentage of the cost to buy a new replacement item) aren't acceptable."

### Cited Findings
- Statute, 26 U.S.C. §170(f)(16)(A), verbatim (LII): "no deduction shall be allowed under subsection (a) for any contribution of clothing or a household item unless such clothing or household item is in good used condition or better." — [26 USC 170](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170)
- §170(f)(16)(B) "Items of minimal value": "the Secretary may by regulation deny a deduction under subsection (a) for any contribution of clothing or a household item which has minimal monetary value." — [26 USC 170](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170)
- §170(f)(16)(C): "Subparagraphs (A) and (B) shall not apply to any contribution of a single item of clothing or a household item for which a deduction of more than $500 is claimed if the taxpayer includes with the taxpayer's return a qualified appraisal with respect to the property." — [26 USC 170](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170)
- §170(f)(16)(D)(i): "household item" "includes furniture, furnishings, electronics, appliances, linens, and other similar items." (D)(ii) excludes "food," "paintings, antiques, and other objects of art," "jewelry and gems," and "collections." — [26 USC 170](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170)
- Pub 526 (2025), "Clothing and Household Items," verbatim: "You can't take a deduction for clothing or household items you donate unless the clothing or household items are in good used condition or better. Exception. You can take a deduction for a contribution of an item of clothing or a household item that isn't in good used condition or better if you deduct more than $500 for it, and include a qualified appraisal prepared by a qualified appraiser and a completed Form 8283, Section B. Household items. Household items include: Furniture and furnishings, Electronics, Appliances, Linens, and Other similar items. Household items don't include: Food; Paintings, antiques, and other objects of art; Jewelry and gems; and Collections. FMV. To determine the FMV of these items, use the rules under Determining FMV, later." — [IRS Pub 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- Pub 526 (2025), "Determining FMV," verbatim: "FMV is the price at which property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither having to buy or sell, and both having reasonable knowledge of all the relevant facts. Used clothing. The FMV of used clothing and other personal items is usually far less than the price you paid for them. There are no fixed formulas or methods for finding the value of items of clothing. You should claim as the value the price that buyers of used items actually pay in used clothing stores, such as consignment or thrift shops. Also, see Clothing and Household Items, earlier. Example. You donated a coat to a thrift store operated by a place of worship. You paid $300 for the coat 3 years ago. Similar coats in the thrift store sell for $50. The FMV of the coat is $50. Your donation is limited to $50." — [IRS Pub 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- Pub 526 (2025), "Household items," verbatim: "The FMV of used household items, such as furniture, appliances, and linens, is usually much lower than the price paid when new. These items may have little or no market value because they are in a worn condition, out of style, or no longer useful. For these reasons, formulas (such as using a percentage of the cost to buy a new replacement item) aren't acceptable in determining value. You should support your valuation with photographs, canceled checks, receipts from your purchase of the items, or other evidence. Magazine or newspaper articles and photographs that describe the items and statements by the recipients of the items are also useful. Don't include any of this evidence with your tax return. If the property is valuable because it is old or unique, see the discussion under Art and Collectibles in Pub. 561." — [IRS Pub 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- Pub 526 (2025), "Article of clothing or household item over $500 not in good used condition," verbatim: "Form 8283, Section B, must be completed and the Form 8283 attached to the tax return if you are contributing a single article of clothing or household item over $500 that is not in good used condition. See the Form 8283 instructions for more information." — [IRS Pub 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- Pub 526 (2025), "What's New," lists only two items for 2025: deductibility of contributions to federally chartered §501(c)(19) veteran service organizations "Beginning in 2025," and the one-time QCD election "up to $54,000." It does not mention the 0.5% AGI floor or the non-itemizer deduction (which take effect in tax year 2026, not 2025). — [IRS Pub 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- Form 8283 instructions (12/2025), "Clothing and household items," verbatim: "The FMV of used household items and clothing is usually much lower than when new. A good measure of value might be the price that buyers of these used items actually pay in consignment or thrift shops. You can also review classified ads in the newspaper or on the Internet to see what similar products sell for. Generally, you cannot claim a deduction for clothing or household items you donate unless the clothing or household items are in good used condition or better. However, you can claim a deduction for a contribution of an item of clothing or a household item that is not in good used condition or better if your claimed value is more than $500 and you substantiate that value with a qualified appraisal and Form 8283, Section B. Both must be included with your return." — [IRS i8283](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8283)

### Inferences
- The Form 8283 instructions' sentence "You can also review classified ads in the newspaper or on the Internet to see what similar products sell for" is the only IRS text found that sanctions online listings as a valuation reference for clothing/household items; it refers to what items "sell for," which supports using *sold* prices (not asking prices) as comparables.
- Pub 526's explicit rejection of "formulas (such as using a percentage of the cost to buy a new replacement item)" means a dataset derived as a fixed percentage of new retail price would not be IRS-consistent; values must trace to observed used-market prices.
- Because "collections," "jewelry and gems," and art/antiques are carved out of "household items," a clothing/household dataset should explicitly exclude or flag those categories (they are governed by Pub 561's Art and Collectibles rules and are often "similar items" groups for the $5,000 appraisal test).

### Gaps
- No Treasury regulation has been issued under §170(f)(16)(B) defining "minimal monetary value," and no regulation or IRS guidance defines "good used condition" (no such definition exists in Pub 526, Pub 561, the Form 8283 instructions, or §1.170A-16 as fetched).

## Q4. Substantiation thresholds (<$250; $250–$500; >$500; >$5,000; >$500,000), Form 8283 Section A columns, and when cost basis can be omitted

### Takeaway
Thresholds are cumulative: a receipt or reliable written records (including condition and "how you figured the FMV") for any noncash gift under $250; a contemporaneous written acknowledgment (CWA) at $250+; Form 8283 Section A (with how/when acquired and cost basis) when the deduction for an item or group of similar items exceeds $500; a qualified appraisal plus Section B when it exceeds $5,000 (aggregating all "similar items" such as all clothing, across all donees); and attachment of the appraisal itself above $500,000. Cost basis may be omitted only for publicly traded securities held >12 months (and Pub 526 says basis of property held ≥12 months is required only "if available"), or with an attached reasonable-cause explanation.

### Cited Findings
- Statute, §170(f)(8)(A): "No deduction shall be allowed under subsection (a) for any contribution of $250 or more unless the taxpayer substantiates the contribution by a contemporaneous written acknowledgment of the contribution by the donee organization". — [26 USC 170](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170)
- Statute, §170(f)(11)(A)(i): no deduction "for any contribution of property for which a deduction of more than $500 is claimed" unless the (B)–(D) requirements are met; (A)(ii)(II): "Clause (i) shall not apply if it is shown that the failure to meet such requirements is due to reasonable cause and not to willful neglect." (B) (>$500): the taxpayer "includes with the return for the taxable year in which the contribution is made a description of such property and such other information as the Secretary may require." (C) (>$5,000): the taxpayer "obtains a qualified appraisal of such property and attaches to the return … such information regarding such property and such appraisal as the Secretary may require." (D) (>$500,000): the taxpayer "attaches to the return for the taxable year a qualified appraisal of such property." (F): "For purposes of determining thresholds under this paragraph, property and all similar items of property donated to 1 or more donees shall be treated as 1 property." — [26 USC 170](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170)
- §170(f)(11)(E)(i) "qualified appraisal" = an appraisal that "is treated for purposes of this paragraph as a qualified appraisal under regulations or other guidance prescribed by the Secretary, and … is conducted by a qualified appraiser in accordance with generally accepted appraisal standards"; (E)(ii) "qualified appraiser" = an individual who "has earned an appraisal designation from a recognized professional appraiser organization or has otherwise met minimum education and experience requirements set forth in regulations prescribed by the Secretary," "regularly performs appraisals for which the individual receives compensation," and meets other requirements; (E)(iii) requires that the individual "demonstrates verifiable education and experience in valuing the type of property subject to the appraisal" and "has not been prohibited from practicing before the Internal Revenue Service". — [26 USC 170](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170)
- Reg. §1.170A-16(a)(1) (less than $250): no deduction unless the donor keeps a receipt from the donee showing the donee's name and address, the date of the contribution, and a description of the property. §1.170A-16(a)(2): if a receipt is impracticable (e.g., an unattended drop site) the donor may keep "reliable written records" that must also include the FMV on the contribution date and the method used to determine it; (a)(2)(iii)(D): "In the case of a contribution of clothing or a household item," the condition of the item. — [26 CFR 1.170A-16](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.170A-16)
- Reg. §1.170A-16(b) ($250 to $500): no deduction "unless the donor substantiates the contribution with a contemporaneous written acknowledgment" meeting §170(f)(8). — [26 CFR 1.170A-16](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.170A-16)
- Reg. §1.170A-16(c) (>$500 to $5,000): donor must (1) obtain a CWA; (2) complete and file "Form 8283 (Section A)"; (3) Section A must include, among other items, "The manner of acquisition (for example, by purchase, gift, bequest, inheritance, or exchange)," the approximate date of acquisition (or completion if created by the donor), and "the cost or other basis, adjusted as provided by section 1016." Exception (c)(3)(iv)(F): basis need not be reported for publicly traded securities that would produce long-term capital gain, unless the donor elected under §170(b)(1)(C)(iii). — [26 CFR 1.170A-16](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.170A-16)
- Reg. §1.170A-16(d) (>$5,000): the donor must (i) obtain a CWA, (ii) "Obtain[] a qualified appraisal, as defined in § 1.170A-17(a)(1)," prepared by a qualified appraiser, and (iii) complete and file Form 8283 (Section B). (d)(2) exceptions (Section A suffices): publicly traded securities; certain intellectual property under §170(e)(1)(B)(iii); certain qualified vehicles with a §170(f)(12) acknowledgment; and inventory/property held primarily for sale to customers. — [26 CFR 1.170A-16](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.170A-16)

#### Case law applying the aggregation and appraisal rules to clothing donations
- Grainger v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2018-117: taxpayer bought discounted Talbots clothing and donated it to Goodwill, claiming $34,401 for 2012 based on original price tags; the court held all items were "similar items of property" under Reg. §1.170A-13(c)(7)(iii), aggregated them, and found the §170(f)(11)(C) qualified-appraisal requirement applied because the total exceeded $5,000; Goodwill receipts that only said clothing was donated were not a valid CWA; the six Forms 8283 were not signed by a Goodwill official; her self-prepared spreadsheet was given no evidentiary weight; the court said "No rational buyer having knowledge of the relevant facts" would have paid more than Talbots' price. — [The Tax Adviser (Oct 2018)](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2018/oct/inflated-charitable-contribution-deduction-denied.html)
- Bass v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2023-41: taxpayer made 173 separate trips to Goodwill and Salvation Army in 2017, self-valued receipts totaling $18,837 (Goodwill) and $11,779 (Salvation Army) including $25,651 of clothing combined, attached two Forms 8283 but no appraisal, arguing no single item exceeded $5,000; the court held similar items must be aggregated across the year, so a qualified appraisal was required, and denied the entire deduction. — [CPA Practice Advisor (May 2023)](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2023/05/16/sizing-up-a-charitable-deduction-for-clothing-when-is-an-appraisal-required/79750/)
- Kunkel v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2015-71: the Tax Court disallowed $37,315 of deductions for gifts of clothing, books and other personal property to Goodwill and other organizations; per a secondary summary, the taxpayers made no effort to keep required records, so no reasonable-cause defense under §6664(c)(1) was available and the §6662(a) penalty applied. (Secondary summaries only; opinion not fetched.) — [Church Law & Tax summary](https://www.churchlawandtax.com/legal-developments/married-couples-37315-charitable-contribution-deduction-disallowed/); [TaxProf Blog](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/?p=7997)
- TIGTA's 2012 report summarizes the attachment rules the same way: taxpayers "must also attach an appraisal for art donations of $20,000 or more, any single item of clothing or any household item that is not in good used condition or better and more than $500, and easements on buildings in historic districts." — [TIGTA 2013-40-009](https://www.tigta.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2022-06/201340009fr_0.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because "clothing" is a named "similar items" category and aggregation is across all donees for the year, a donor whose year's clothing donations total more than $5,000 at the dataset's values needs a qualified appraisal regardless of how many drop-offs or charities were involved; a valuation site should warn users of this cliff (and separately for "furniture," "electronic equipment," "household appliances," "toys," "everyday kitchenware," "china," "crystal," "silver," and "books," which are each named categories).
- The "Thrift shop value" entry in Form 8283 column (i) is the IRS-sanctioned label for the method a thrift-price-based dataset implements; a dataset that documents its comparable-sales basis also supports the "method utilized in determining the fair market value" record that §1.170A-13(b)(2)(ii)(D) and §1.170A-16(a)(2) require.
- Cost basis is routinely unavailable for old clothing; Pub 526's "if available" language for property held 12 months or more, plus the instructions' reasonable-cause note, means donors can file Section A without basis for long-held clothing if they attach an explanation, but the regulation itself ((c)(3)(iv)) only exempts publicly traded securities, so this is a practice/instructions accommodation rather than a regulatory exemption.

### Gaps
- §1.170A-16(c)(3) as summarized by the fetch did not show a "reasonable cause" exception for omitted basis; the accommodation appears in the Form 8283 instructions and Pub 526 ("if available"), not in the regulation text I could confirm.
- The Kunkel opinion itself was not fetched; details come from secondary summaries.

## Q5. Does the IRS endorse, accept, or reference any third-party valuation guide (Salvation Army, Goodwill, ItsDeductible) or eBay sold prices?

### Takeaway
No. Across Pub 561 (12/2025), Pub 526 (2025), the Form 8283 and its instructions (12/2025), and Tax Topic 506, the IRS names no third-party clothing/household valuation guide and never mentions eBay or ItsDeductible; the closest official language is Form 8283's column (i) example "Thrift shop value," the instructions' statement that donors "can also review classified ads in the newspaper or on the Internet to see what similar products sell for," and Pub 561's general warning that price guides "are not always reliable indicators of FMV and should be supported by other evidence."

### Cited Findings
- Pub 561 (12/2025) full text contains no mention of Salvation Army/Goodwill valuation guides, ItsDeductible, or eBay; the only mention of the Salvation Army is as the donee in the FMV example. — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Pub 526 (2025) full text contains no mention of any third-party valuation guide, ItsDeductible, or eBay. — [IRS Pub 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- Form 8283 instructions (12/2025): column (i) examples include "'Thrift shop value' (for clothing or household items)"; the clothing/household paragraph says "A good measure of value might be the price that buyers of these used items actually pay in consignment or thrift shops. You can also review classified ads in the newspaper or on the Internet to see what similar products sell for." No guide is named. — [IRS i8283](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8283)
- Pub 561's generic caution on published price guides (collectibles context): "These sources are not always reliable indicators of FMV and should be supported by other evidence." and on vehicle guides: "The prices are not 'official,' and these publications are not considered an appraisal of any specific donated property. But they do provide clues for making an appraisal". — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Pub 561's only treatment of auction prices: "The price an item sold for in an auction may have been the result of a rigged sale," and appraisers may consider "works of art sold at auction in arm's-length sales" for time adjustments. — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- A targeted web search for IRS material mentioning "ItsDeductible" or "eBay" together with FMV returned no IRS document; results were tax-software, charity, and blog sources. — [search summary, e.g., Washington Secretary of State guidance](https://www.sos.wa.gov/_assets/charities/donations-of-clothes-and-household-items.pdf)
- A 1998 Chronicle of Philanthropy piece quoting the then-current Pub 561 shows the "does not lend itself to fixed formulas or methods" / consignment-or-thrift-shop language has been in Pub 561 for at least 28 years and does not name any charity guide. — [Chronicle of Philanthropy (1998)](https://www.philanthropy.com/opinion/what-the-irs-says-about-how-taxpayers-should-value-gifts-of-property/)
- The Form 8283 donee acknowledgment (Part V) states: "This acknowledgment does not represent agreement with the claimed fair market value." — [IRS f8283.pdf](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f8283.pdf)
- Court treatment: in Grainger the court gave "no evidentiary value" to the taxpayer's self-prepared valuation spreadsheet and applied the willing-buyer test; in Bass self-valued charity receipts totaling over $5,000 of clothing did not avoid the appraisal requirement. — [The Tax Adviser](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2018/oct/inflated-charitable-contribution-deduction-denied.html); [CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2023/05/16/sizing-up-a-charitable-deduction-for-clothing-when-is-an-appraisal-required/79750/)

### Inferences
- The absence of any IRS endorsement cuts both ways: a new open dataset is on equal footing with the Salvation Army/Goodwill guides in terms of official status (none), and its defensibility rests entirely on whether its values are traceable to actual prices "buyers of used items actually pay" in thrift/consignment markets, with documented method, date, market, and condition — i.e., whether it functions as the "other evidence" Pub 561 demands.
- eBay or other online *sold* prices fit the Form 8283 instructions' "classified ads … on the Internet to see what similar products sell for" language and Pub 561's comparable-sales factors (similarity, timing, arm's length, same market area, normal market conditions), but Pub 561's auction caution means sold-price data should be screened for non-arm's-length or anomalous sales and should be disclosed as online-resale comparables rather than thrift-store prices where the two markets diverge.

### Gaps
- No IRS Chief Counsel advice, Internal Revenue Manual section, audit technique guide, or training material addressing clothing/household valuation guides or eBay prices was found in this research; I did not search the IRM or CCA databases directly, so absence is not confirmed.
- No Tax Court opinion was found that evaluates the accuracy of a Salvation Army/Goodwill guide or an eBay-based valuation on the merits; the clothing cases found were decided on substantiation (CWA, Form 8283 completeness, aggregation/appraisal) rather than on the valuation method.

## Q6. Penalties relevant to overvaluation: IRC §6662 (150%/200%), §6664(c)(3), and §6695A

### Takeaway
A 20% accuracy-related penalty applies when claimed value is 150% or more of the correct value and the resulting underpayment exceeds $5,000; it rises to 40% at 200% or more. For charitable deduction property there is no reasonable-cause defense to a gross misstatement at all, and for a substantial misstatement only if the value was based on a qualified appraisal plus a good-faith investigation. Appraisers face a separate §6695A penalty.

### Cited Findings
- §6662(a): "there shall be added to the tax an amount equal to 20 percent of the portion of the underpayment to which this section applies." §6662(b)(3): applies to "Any substantial valuation misstatement under chapter 1." — [26 USC 6662](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662)
- §6662(e)(1)(A): a substantial valuation misstatement exists if "the value of any property (or the adjusted basis of any property) claimed on any return of tax imposed by chapter 1 is 150 percent or more of the amount determined to be the correct amount of such valuation or adjusted basis". §6662(e)(2): no penalty "unless the portion of the underpayment for the taxable year attributable to substantial valuation misstatements under chapter 1 exceeds $5,000 ($10,000 in the case of a corporation other than an S corporation or a personal holding company)." — [26 USC 6662](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662)
- §6662(h)(1): for gross valuation misstatements "subsection (a) shall be applied with respect to such portion by substituting '40 percent' for '20 percent'"; §6662(h)(2)(A)(i): a gross misstatement is determined by substituting "'200 percent' for '150 percent'" in (e)(1)(A). — [26 USC 6662](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662)
- §6662(l) (as shown on LII): for "overstatements of the deduction provided in section 170(p)" (the new non-itemizer charitable deduction) the penalty is applied by substituting "50 percent" for "20 percent". — [26 USC 6662](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662)
- §6664(c)(1): "No penalty shall be imposed under section 6662 or 6663 with respect to any portion of an underpayment if it is shown that there was a reasonable cause for such portion and that the taxpayer acted in good faith with respect to such portion." §6664(c)(3): "In the case of any underpayment attributable to a substantial or gross valuation overstatement under chapter 1 with respect to charitable deduction property, paragraph (1) shall not apply. The preceding sentence shall not apply to a substantial valuation overstatement under chapter 1 if— (A) the claimed value of the property was based on a qualified appraisal made by a qualified appraiser, and (B) in addition to obtaining such appraisal, the taxpayer made a good faith investigation of the value of the contributed property." §6664(c)(4)(A): "charitable deduction property" means "any property contributed by the taxpayer in a contribution for which a deduction was claimed under section 170," excluding publicly quoted securities for purposes of (3). — [26 USC 6664](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6664)
- §6695A(a): penalty on a person who prepares an appraisal that the person "knows, or reasonably should have known," would be used in connection with a return or claim for refund, where the claimed value based on the appraisal results in a substantial or gross valuation misstatement. §6695A(b): the penalty is the lesser of (1) the greater of 10 percent of the underpayment attributable to the misstatement or $1,000, or (2) 125 percent of the gross income received by the appraiser for the appraisal. §6695A(c): "No penalty shall be imposed under subsection (a)" if the appraiser establishes to the Secretary's satisfaction that "the value established in the appraisal was more likely than not the proper value." (LII page shows subsections (a)–(c) only.) — [26 USC 6695A](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6695A)
- Pub 526 (2025), "Penalty," verbatim: "You may be liable for an accuracy-related penalty under section 6662 if you overstate the value or adjusted basis of contributed property. 20% penalty. The penalty is 20% of the amount by which you underpaid your tax because of the overstatement, if: The value or adjusted basis claimed on your return is 150% or more of the correct amount, and You underpaid your tax by more than $5,000 because of the overstatement. 40% penalty. The penalty is 40%, rather than 20%, if: The value or adjusted basis claimed on your return is 200% or more of the correct amount, and You underpaid your tax by more than $5,000 because of the overstatement." — [IRS Pub 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- Pub 561 (12/2025) "Penalty" section carries the same 150%/200% and $5,000 thresholds (quoted in Q2 above). — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)
- Pub 561 "Prohibited appraisal fee": "Generally, no part of the fee arrangement for a qualified appraisal can be based on a percentage of the appraised value of the property." — [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)

### Inferences
- The $5,000-underpayment trigger makes the §6662 valuation penalty unlikely for a typical clothing/household donor (it would require roughly $15,000–$20,000+ of excess deduction at ordinary marginal rates), so for most users the practical risk is deduction disallowance for substantiation failures (Form 8283/CWA/appraisal), not the penalty; for high-volume donors the penalty and the loss of the reasonable-cause defense under §6664(c)(3) are real.
- §6664(c)(3) means that for a substantial misstatement the only safe harbor is a qualified appraisal plus a good-faith investigation; reliance on any valuation guide (including a new one) is not itself a statutory defense, so the dataset should be positioned as evidence supporting a good-faith investigation, not as a safe harbor.
- §6695A does not reach a website or dataset publisher who does not "prepare an appraisal" for a specific return; it targets appraisers.

### Gaps
- I did not verify which OBBBA section added §6662(l) or its effective date; the LII text shows the provision exists.

## Q7. 2024–2026 legislative/regulatory changes affecting noncash contributions (OBBBA, Pub. L. 119-21, enacted July 4, 2025)

### Takeaway
For tax years beginning after December 31, 2025, OBBBA (1) adds a permanent above-the-line deduction for non-itemizers of up to $1,000 ($2,000 joint) for *cash* contributions only (new §170(p)), and (2) imposes a 0.5%-of-contribution-base floor on itemized charitable deductions (new §170(b)(1)(I)). Neither changes the FMV, condition, or substantiation rules for clothing/household gifts, but together they change who needs a valuation: non-itemizers get nothing for noncash gifts, and itemizers lose the first 0.5% of AGI of their total giving.

### Cited Findings
- Statutory effective-date notes on LII for Pub. L. 119-21 §70425(c) (139 Stat. 237), §70426(d) (139 Stat. 238), and §70429(b) (139 Stat. 239), each: "The amendments made by this section [amending this section] shall apply to taxable years beginning after December 31, 2025." (The note for the non-itemizer section was cut off in the fetch but secondary sources uniformly report the same 2026 effective date.) — [26 USC 170 (LII notes)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170)
- §170(p) (as shown on LII): the deduction for an individual who does not elect to itemize is capped at "$1,000 ($2,000 in the case of a joint return)" and counts only "contributions made in cash during such taxable year"; contributions to a §509(a)(3) supporting organization or "for the establishment of a new, or maintenance of an existing, donor advised fund" are excluded; the amendment note shows the amounts were changed from "$300 ($600" to "$1,000 ($2,000". — [26 USC 170](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170)
- §170(b)(1)(I) "0.5-percent floor" (as shown on LII): charitable contributions of an individual "shall be allowed only to the extent that the aggregate of such contributions exceeds 0.5 percent of the taxpayer's contribution base for the taxable year," followed by ordering clauses (i)–(vi) that apply the floor first against subparagraph (D) contributions and last against subparagraph (G) (60% cash) contributions; the LII amendment note says the 2025 law "added subpar. (I)." — [26 USC 170](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170)
- Secondary summaries (law firms, CPA journals, planned-giving offices) consistently describe: non-itemizers may deduct up to $1,000/$2,000 of cash gifts to public charities (not DAFs or private non-operating foundations) starting in 2026; itemizers may deduct only giving above 0.5% of AGI; the floor does not apply to the non-itemizer deduction; the 60%-of-AGI limit for cash gifts is made permanent. — [Taft Law](https://www.taftlaw.com/news-events/law-bulletins/charitable-giving-after-the-obbba-the-2026-outlook/); [Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/it/insights/2025/10/new-limitations-on-charitable-deductions-take-effect-in-2026); [CPA Journal](https://www.cpajournal.com/2025/12/30/changes-to-charitable-giving-and-planning-to-consider-before-year-end/); [Fredrikson](https://www.fredlaw.com/lets-talk-about-tax/giving-with-limits-how-obbba-reshaped-charitable-deductions)
- Carryover treatment of amounts disallowed by the floor is described inconsistently in secondary sources: one says pre-2026 carryovers are not subject to the floor when used later ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/it/insights/2025/10/new-limitations-on-charitable-deductions-take-effect-in-2026)); others say floor-disallowed amounts carry forward subject to the same floor ([Fredrikson](https://www.fredlaw.com/lets-talk-about-tax/giving-with-limits-how-obbba-reshaped-charitable-deductions); [CPA Journal](https://www.cpajournal.com/2025/12/30/changes-to-charitable-giving-and-planning-to-consider-before-year-end/)). The LII text indicates carryover of floor-disallowed amounts is handled in §170(d)(1)(C) and applies only in years when an AGI-percentage limit is also exceeded. — [26 USC 170](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170)
- Pub 526 (2025) "What's New" lists only the §501(c)(19) veteran-organization change ("Beginning in 2025") and the one-time $54,000 QCD election; the 2026 changes will appear in the 2026 edition. — [IRS Pub 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- Reg. §1.170A-16 (substantiation) was last amended effective for taxable years ending on or after November 20, 2023 (paragraphs (c)(3)(v), (d)(3)(viii)–(ix), (f)(4) and (6)); no 2024–2026 amendments to the clothing/household rules were found. — [26 CFR 1.170A-16](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.170A-16)
- Form 8283 and its instructions were revised December 2025 (form "Created 7/1/25"); Pub 561 was revised December 2025. — [IRS f8283.pdf](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f8283.pdf); [IRS Pub 561](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p561)

### Inferences
- Starting with 2026 returns (filed in 2027), the population that benefits from a clothing/household valuation is itemizers whose total 2026 giving exceeds 0.5% of AGI (e.g., >$500 at $100k AGI); a $300 bag of clothes from a non-itemizer or from an itemizer below the floor yields no federal deduction, which reduces how often small valuations matter but does not reduce the substantiation burden for those who do deduct.
- The non-itemizer deduction is cash-only, so DonationValues.org should state plainly that noncash (clothing/household) gifts never qualify for the §170(p) deduction.
- The 0.5% floor increases the attractiveness of "bunching" noncash donations into one year, which in turn makes it more likely a donor crosses the $5,000 "similar items" appraisal threshold for clothing in a single year — a warning the site should surface.

### Gaps
- The enrolled-bill section numbers for the non-itemizer deduction vs. the 0.5% floor (commonly reported as §70424 and §70425) were not confirmed against the Statutes at Large text; only the LII effective-date notes were read.
- Whether the IRS will re-issue regulations under §170(f)(16)(B) ("minimal monetary value") or otherwise change clothing/household rules for 2026 is unknown; nothing was found.

## Q8. TIGTA/GAO reports on noncash charitable contribution compliance

### Takeaway
TIGTA's December 2012 report (2013-40-009) is the principal audit: in statistical samples of Tax Year 2010 returns claiming more than $5,000 of noncash contributions, about 60% (303 of 507) did not comply with reporting requirements, and TIGTA estimated more than 273,000 taxpayers claimed about $3.8 billion of potentially unsubstantiated noncash contributions, reducing tax by about $1.1 billion. No GAO report specific to clothing/household noncash contributions and no TIGTA report after 2012 on this topic was found.

### Cited Findings
- TIGTA, "Many Taxpayers Are Still Not Complying With Noncash Charitable Contribution Reporting Requirements," Ref. No. 2013-40-009 (Dec. 2012): "In Processing Years 2011 and 2012, approximately 21 million and 20 million individuals claimed noncash charitable contributions, respectively." (footnote: "Errors in IRS data prevented us from being able to compute the amount of noncash charitable contributions claimed.") — [TIGTA 2013-40-009](https://www.tigta.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2022-06/201340009fr_0.pdf)
- Same report: "Approximately 60 percent (303 of 507) of individuals in our statistical samples did not comply with the reporting requirements. These individuals claimed approximately $201.6 million in noncash charitable contributions." Figure 1: for contributions of $5,000–$500,000, 287 returns reviewed, 213 exceptions (74% error rate); for contributions over $500,000, 220 reviewed, 90 exceptions (41%). Exception issues: "Proper Forms Not Attached" 16/39; "Incorrectly Completed Form 8283" 116/59; "Incorrectly Reported Items on Form 8283" 129/10; "Unclear Description of Donated Property" 17/29. — [TIGTA 2013-40-009](https://www.tigta.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2022-06/201340009fr_0.pdf)
- Same report: "We estimate that more than 273,000 taxpayers claimed approximately $3.8 billion in potentially unsubstantiated noncash contributions in Tax Year 2010, which resulted in an estimated $1.1 billion reduction in tax." Errors included "Forms 8283 or appraisals not attached when required," "Incorrectly reported items on Section A of Form 8283 by not combining similar items," and "Unclear or incomplete descriptions of the donated property." "None of the tax returns in our samples had been examined." — [TIGTA 2013-40-009](https://www.tigta.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2022-06/201340009fr_0.pdf)
- Same report, sampling: four statistically valid samples (150 e-filed and 150 paper returns with contributions of $5,000–$500,000; 130 e-filed and 120 paper returns over $500,000), 90% confidence, ±3% precision. — [TIGTA 2013-40-009](https://www.tigta.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2022-06/201340009fr_0.pdf)
- Same report, background: the Joint Committee on Taxation (Jan 2005) had proposed "Limiting the tax deduction for charitable contributions of clothing and household items" (a $500/year cap, per secondary sources), which Congress did not enact; Congress instead enacted the "good used condition" rule in the Pension Protection Act of 2006. — [TIGTA 2013-40-009](https://www.tigta.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2022-06/201340009fr_0.pdf); [Senate Finance (Grassley 2007)](https://www.finance.senate.gov/ranking-members-news/grassley-expresses-concern-over-unsubstantiated-noncash-charitable-contributions)
- Earlier TIGTA report 2007-30-049 (March 5, 2007) found "approximately 60 percent of taxpayers who claim large-dollar noncash charitable contributions may not be complying with Federal reporting requirements"; the IRS agreed to flag returns claiming more than $500 without a Form 8283. The 2012 report says its findings "echoed TIGTA's previous examinations of noncompliance in 2007 and 2009." — [Accounting Today summary](https://www.accountingtoday.com/news/tigta-better-checks-needed-on-non-cash-donations); [TIGTA 2013-40-009](https://www.tigta.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2022-06/201340009fr_0.pdf)
- TIGTA's 2012 IRS examination data: one project examining 219 returns over four years closed 28% with no change and 72% (158 of 219) with assessments totaling more than $5 million. — [TIGTA 2013-40-009](https://www.tigta.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2022-06/201340009fr_0.pdf)
- Searches for a GAO report on noncash (clothing/household) contribution compliance returned no GAO product; results were TIGTA, JCT, and Senate Finance materials. — [search results](https://www.finance.senate.gov/ranking-members-news/grassley-expresses-concern-over-unsubstantiated-noncash-charitable-contributions)
- Searches for TIGTA reports in 2023–2025 on noncash contributions/Form 8283 returned nothing newer than 2013-40-009. — [search results](https://www.tigta.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2022-06/201340009fr_0.pdf)

### Inferences
- TIGTA's samples were of returns over $5,000, so its 60% noncompliance figure describes large-dollar donors (appraisal/Section B failures), not the typical sub-$5,000 clothing donor; its finding that errors included "not combining similar items" directly reflects the aggregation trap in Q4.
- The 20–21 million individuals claiming noncash contributions in 2011–2012 (pre-TCJA) compares with 6.5 million returns claiming the noncash deduction on Schedule A in TY2022 (Q9), reflecting the post-2018 collapse in itemizing; the OBBBA floor will shrink the population further.

### Gaps
- No GAO report on clothing/household noncash contributions was found; GAO's work on vehicle donations (mid-2000s) was not pursued as out of scope.
- No compliance statistics specific to clothing/household items (as opposed to all noncash contributions over $5,000) were found in any TIGTA or GAO product.

## Q9. IRS Statistics of Income data: returns claiming noncash contributions and typical amounts for clothing and household goods (Form 8283)

### Takeaway
SOI's Form 8283 data (latest: Tax Year 2023, released June 2026) show about 2.79 million returns with Form 8283 claiming $93.7 billion; clothing appears on about 1.84 million returns (4.32 million donations, $5.8 billion, average $3,152 per return and $1,343 per donation) and household items on about 1.19 million returns (2.69 million donations, $4.39 billion, $3,676 per return, $1,634 per donation). Clothing and household items are ~80% of all reported noncash donations by count but only ~11% by dollar amount.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- Only returns with Form 8283 (i.e., noncash deductions over $500) appear in SOI's donation-type data; the 6.5 million Schedule A noncash claimants in TY2022 vs. 2.9 million Form 8283 filers implies roughly 3.6 million returns claimed $500 or less of noncash gifts with no itemized reporting — the population least likely to be audited but most in need of a simple, defensible valuation.
- Average clothing amounts per Form 8283 return (~$3,000) are below the $5,000 "similar items" appraisal threshold, but the distribution is skewed; a meaningful minority of clothing donors (not quantified in Figure A) exceed $5,000 and need appraisals. Tables 1A/4 (by AGI and age) would allow a distributional estimate but were not parsed.
- The per-donation averages (~$1,200–$1,600 for a clothing or household "donation" line on Form 8283) describe a bag or carload of items, not single garments; per-item values in a dataset will be far smaller.

### Gaps
- No SOI breakdown by individual clothing/household item type or per-item values exists; Form 8283 captures a description and lump amount per donation line.
- Tables 1A–5 (AGI, donee type, age) for 2023 were not parsed; the share of clothing donors over $5,000 is therefore not quantified here.
